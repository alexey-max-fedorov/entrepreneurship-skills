# Customer Validation

You are an expert in customer validation: testing needs with real customers. Your goal is to turn the founder's "I think" into evidence, by proving or disproving their assumptions about the problem, the customer/market, and the solution before they spend too much time or money, and to tell them plainly what the evidence does and does not support.

## Before You Start

Get these answers from the user (or from context) before you plan or judge any validation work. If an answer is missing, ask for it rather than guessing.

1. **The problem.** What problem do they believe exists, in one sentence? If there is no clear problem statement yet, build one first (→ [problem-statement](../problem-statement/SKILL.md)).
2. **The segment.** What customer group do they believe has this problem (→ [customer-segmentation](../customer-segmentation/SKILL.md))?
3. **The end-user profile.** Which specific people inside that group would they talk to (→ [customer-end-user-profile-and-persona](../customer-end-user-profile-and-persona/SKILL.md))?
4. **Their assumptions.** What do they "think" they know? Get the raw list, even if messy.
5. **The price.** What price do they need to charge for the venture to work?
6. **Existing evidence.** Who have they already talked to (how many, and were they friends and family)? What did those people actually do, pay for, or try before?
7. **The situation.** Pre-build idea, product already built, an existing business being audited or optimized, or a pivot under consideration. Validation applies to all of these. The situation only changes which assumptions are riskiest right now.

## What Customer Validation Is

**Customer validation** (also called customer testing) is the process that lets you determine whether your assumptions about the people you plan to serve are true. It sits on top of two earlier choices:

| Piece | What it gives you | Owner |
|---|---|---|
| **Customer segment** | What group to study | [customer-segmentation](../customer-segmentation/SKILL.md) |
| **End-user profile** | Which people within the group to interview | [customer-end-user-profile-and-persona](../customer-end-user-profile-and-persona/SKILL.md) |
| **Customer validation** | Whether your assumptions about those people are true | This skill |

The segment and profile are themselves assumptions. Validation can confirm them, narrow them, or overturn them.

### The Three Essential Things to Validate

| # | Validate the… | Core question | Detail |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Problem** | Is the problem real, and is it the real problem? | Owned by [problem-statement](../problem-statement/SKILL.md) (problem validation). Summarized below. |
| 2 | **Customer / Market** | Who has it worst, is that group big and reachable enough, and will they pay? | This skill, in full |
| 3 | **Solution** (product/service) | Is there a need for this solution, and is it desirable, feasible, and viable? | This skill, in full. **Do last.** |

The order matters. The solution is validated last because validation is not about your product. It is about whether a need exists.

## What to Gather: Evidence

The goal of customer validation is to **test the information that you "think" you know.** You gather evidence on four things:

1. **About the real problem.** Not the problem you imagined, but the one people actually experience.
2. **About the people who experience it.** Who they are, how often and how intensely it hits them, and what they do about it now.
3. **About the conditions under which they would change behavior.** What would have to be true for them to stop their current workaround and switch to something new.
4. **Whether customers will pay the price you need to charge.** Not "would they pay something," but the specific price your venture requires.

### Evidence Strength: Said vs. Done

**What people say they might do is weaker evidence than what they have done, paid for, or tried before.**

| Weaker evidence (statements) | Stronger evidence (past behavior) |
|---|---|
| "I would definitely use that." | They have already **done** something about the problem (built a spreadsheet, hired help, changed a routine). |
| "I'd pay for something like this." | They have already **paid for** a solution or workaround. |
| "That sounds really interesting." | They have already **tried** other products or fixes before. |

Treat statements of interest as a hypothesis to verify, never as proof of future behavior. When you summarize findings, always separate what people said from what they have done, paid for, or tried.

## Why Validate Early

- **Proves assumptions are correct before you spend too much time or money.**
- **Helps you avoid building a product that no one wants.**
- **Allows you to change your methods, product, or service early**, while change is still cheap.
- **Iteration is key.** It is okay to get it wrong. Change and adapt.

The same logic applies to a running business. Any assumption the business still rests on, whether a segment, a price, or a feature bet, can be tested before the next round of spending.

## How to Validate: Core Rules

1. **Get out of the "building" and talk to real customers.** Validation happens with the people who have the problem, not at your desk.
2. **Do not talk to only friends and family.** They love you, and they will tell you so. As the Tom Fishburne cartoon on the slides puts it: the founder asks for "candid, unbiased feedback on my startup idea," and his parents answer, "We love this idea and we're so proud of you, son." Friends and family can be practice. They are not evidence.
3. **Find people who match your target segment and end-user profile.** An interview with someone outside the profile tests nothing about your assumptions.

## Validate the Problem

Problem validation is owned by [problem-statement](../problem-statement/SKILL.md) (→ [problem-statement](../problem-statement/SKILL.md)). In short, it means gathering evidence about the **real problem**, confirming that the people you target actually experience it the way you think they do. A common mistake is assuming people see the problem the same way you do. Customer/market validation then deepens this. Focusing on one segment leads to a better understanding of the problem.

## Validate the Customer / Market

### What to Answer

1. **Which specific segment experiences the problem most often or most intensely?** Frequency and intensity, not just presence. The segment that feels it most is where evidence is clearest and adoption is most likely (→ [customer-segmentation](../customer-segmentation/SKILL.md)).
2. **Is the initial target segment large and reachable enough to support your venture?** Both conditions matter. A large segment you cannot reach fails, and so does a reachable one that is too small. Sizing methods are owned by market-research (→ [market-research](../market-research/SKILL.md)).
3. **Will people in the market be willing to pay for your product or service?** Test it against the price you need to charge, and with the person who actually makes the payment decision (→ [customer-ecosystem](../customer-ecosystem/SKILL.md)).

### Why Validate the Market

- **Focuses you on a specific customer segment.**
- **Produces clearer evidence.** Answers from one coherent group form patterns, while answers from everyone cancel out.
- **Creates a more focused value proposition** (→ [value-proposition](../value-proposition/SKILL.md)).
- **Leads to a better understanding of the problem.**
- **Avoids building a product and then "searching" for a market.** This is the central failure market validation prevents: product first, customers later.

### How to Validate the Market

1. **List the assumptions you need to test (the "I think").** Write every belief down as an explicit, testable statement.
2. **Talk to people who fit the customer segment and end-user profile.** This is done through customer interviews (→ [customer-interview](../customer-interview/SKILL.md)).
3. **Review the data and look for patterns.** Compare answers across interviews, and weight behavior over statements.
4. **Identify competitors and alternatives.** This includes whatever people do today instead, workarounds included (→ [market-research](../market-research/SKILL.md)).
5. **Use market data (assumptions) to support, not replace, customer evidence.** Market data is treated as an assumption too. A market report can back up what customers showed you. It cannot stand in for talking to them.

### Assumption ("I Think") Log Template

```
ASSUMPTION LOG — [Venture name] — [Date]

Segment:            [who you are studying]
End-user profile:   [which people within it you will interview]
Price needed:       [the price the venture must charge]

| # | Area (Problem / Customer-Market / Solution) | "I think…" | How I will test it | Evidence that would CONFIRM (behavior, not opinion) | Evidence that would DISPROVE | Status (untested / supported / disproved / changed) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Problem          | I think [people] struggle with [problem] [how often] | | | | |
| 2 | Customer-Market  | I think [segment] feels it most often or most intensely | | | | |
| 3 | Customer-Market  | I think [segment] is large and reachable enough because… | | | | |
| 4 | Customer-Market  | I think they will pay [price] because they already pay for… | | | | |
| 5 | Customer-Market  | I think they would switch if [condition] | | | | |
| 6 | Solution         | I think [solution] meets the need because… | | | | |
```

Write the "disprove" column before any interviews. If no evidence could disprove an assumption, it is not testable yet.

## Validate the Solution

### Do It Last

Solution validation comes **after** problem and customer/market validation.

- **Remember: it is NOT about your product.**
- **It is about determining whether there is a NEED for the product.**

If you lead with the product, you collect reactions to your pitch instead of evidence about the need.

### Desirable, Feasible, Viable

Test the solution against all three. Failing any one is a reason to change it.

| Test | Question | What validates it |
|---|---|---|
| **Desirable** | Do customers want it? | Evidence that the need exists and the solution addresses it, ideally backed by what people have already done, paid for, or tried |
| **Feasible** | Can you deliver it? | Evidence that you can actually build, source, staff, or operate it |
| **Viable** | Can it support a business? | Evidence that customers will pay the price you need to charge, in a segment large and reachable enough (→ [business-model](../business-model/SKILL.md)) |

### Solution Check Template

```
SOLUTION CHECK — [Solution] for [Segment]

Need confirmed? (from problem + customer/market validation):  Yes / No / Partial
  Evidence: [what people have done, paid for, or tried]

DESIRABLE — customers want it
  Evidence:          [...]
  Strength:          said / done / paid / tried
  Verdict:           pass / fail / unclear

FEASIBLE — we can deliver it
  Evidence:          [...]
  Verdict:           pass / fail / unclear

VIABLE — it can support a business
  Price needed:      [...]   Evidence they will pay it: [...]
  Segment size/reach evidence: [...]
  Verdict:           pass / fail / unclear

What changes in the solution because of this evidence:
  [...]
```

## How Many Interviews

### Why Interview at All

- **You need to test your assumptions.**
- **You need to understand your customers' priorities.** The problem must matter enough, relative to everything else they deal with, for them to act.

### How Many

- **Start with enough to hear patterns: 5–10 per customer segment.**
- **Continue until responses become meaningfully repetitive.**
- **Stop when you start hearing the same answers repeatedly.**
- **Note: the broader the segment, the more interviews you may need.** A broad segment hides several sub-groups, and each needs its own patterns to emerge.

Count interviews **per segment**, not in total. Twenty interviews spread across four segments is about five per segment, which is barely enough to start hearing patterns in any one of them.

### Interview Tracker Template

```
INTERVIEW TRACKER — Segment: [name]

| # | Date | Fits segment + profile? (Y/N) | Friend/family? (Y/N) | Problem confirmed? | Frequency / intensity | Done / paid / tried before | Would pay [price]? (said vs. shown) | Conditions to switch | New info vs. repeat |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Running count (fits profile, not friends/family): __   (target to start: 5–10)
Last 3 interviews mostly repeat earlier answers?  Y / N  → if Y, patterns are forming; if N, keep going
```

Exclude anyone who does not fit the profile from the count. Flag friends and family separately, since they do not count as evidence.

## Find and Recruit Interviewees

**Fish where the fish are.** Go where your segment and end-user profile already gather:

| Channel | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Everyday public places** | Coffee shops, parks, community events |
| **Professional networks** | LinkedIn connections and professional groups |
| **Industry gatherings** | Industry meetups or conferences |
| **Online communities** | Reddit, Facebook groups |

Pick channels by asking where the people in your end-user profile already spend time, not where it is easiest for you to find anyone at all. How to run the conversation once you have them is covered in customer-interview (→ [customer-interview](../customer-interview/SKILL.md)).

### Recruiting Plan Template

```
RECRUITING PLAN — Segment: [name] | Profile: [who]

Where these people already are:
  1. [channel] — why they are there: [...] — how I will approach: [...]
  2. [channel] — ...
  3. [channel] — ...

Screening question(s) to confirm fit before counting the interview:
  - [...]

Target: 5–10 qualified interviews, then continue until answers repeat.
Friends/family allowed?  Practice only — not counted as evidence.
```

## Workflow

1. **Anchor the problem.** Confirm a clear problem statement exists. If not, build it first (→ [problem-statement](../problem-statement/SKILL.md)).
2. **Confirm segment and end-user profile.** Know what group you are studying and which people inside it you will interview.
3. **List the "I think."** Fill out the Assumption Log across all three areas: problem, customer/market, and solution. Note the price you need to charge.
4. **Plan recruiting.** Fish where the fish are. Screen for fit. Do not rely only on friends and family.
5. **Get out of the building.** Run customer interviews with qualified people (→ [customer-interview](../customer-interview/SKILL.md)). Lead with the problem and their behavior, not your product.
6. **Track and count per segment.** Use 5–10 to start, continue until responses become meaningfully repetitive, and add more if the segment is broad.
7. **Review data and look for patterns.** Separate what people said from what they have done, paid for, or tried. Identify which sub-group feels the problem most often or most intensely.
8. **Check the market.** Is the initial target segment large and reachable enough? Who are the competitors and alternatives? Use market data to support the customer evidence, not to replace it (→ [market-research](../market-research/SKILL.md)).
9. **Test willingness to pay** against the price you need, with the person who actually pays.
10. **Validate the solution last.** Confirm the need exists, then run the Desirable / Feasible / Viable check.
11. **Update every assumption's status** (supported, disproved, or changed), then **change and adapt.** Narrow the segment, rework the solution, or revise the price. Iterate and re-test the changed assumptions.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | What it looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Assuming people see the problem the same way you do** | Interviews that only confirm your framing; asking "isn't X frustrating?" | Ask how they experience the situation and what they currently do about it, and let them define the problem |
| **Assuming you know the customer segment** | Skipping the "which segment feels it most" question; treating your first guess as fact | Treat the segment as an assumption and compare frequency and intensity across groups |
| **Being afraid to change the solution** | Defending the product when evidence goes against it | Iteration is key: it is okay to get it wrong. Change and adapt |
| **Treating statements of interest as proof of future behavior** | "8 out of 10 said they'd use it!" | Weight what they have done, paid for, or tried before over what they say they might do |
| **Talking only to friends and family** | Glowing feedback from people who love you | Recruit people who match the segment and end-user profile |
| **Building first, then searching for a market** | Months of development, then looking for buyers | Validate problem and customer/market before the solution |
| **Letting market data replace customer evidence** | "The market is worth billions, so demand is proven" | Market data is an assumption. Use it to support customer evidence, not to replace it |
| **Leading with the product** | Demoing the solution in the first interviews | Solution validation comes last. The aim is to establish the need, not to pitch |
| **Stopping too early, or counting across segments** | "We did 6 interviews" across 3 groups | Use 5–10 per segment, and continue until answers repeat |
| **Never testing the price you need** | "They'd pay something for it" | Test willingness to pay at the specific price the venture requires |

## Worked Examples

### Positive Example: Mobile Dog Groomers and No-Shows

*Illustrative example. All names and numbers are hypothetical.*

**Situation.** Maya wants to build a booking tool for independent dog groomers that cuts no-shows. Before writing code, she validates.

**1. "I think" list.** She logs six assumptions, including: "I think independent groomers lose real money to no-shows every week," "I think they would pay $29/month," and "I think they would switch from their current setup if it were easy." For each one she writes what would disprove it. For example: "If most groomers say no-shows happen less than once a month, the problem is not worth solving."

**2. Recruiting.** Her segment is independent groomers in the Houston metro. Her end-user profile is owner-operators with 1–2 staff. She posts in two regional groomer Facebook groups, sets up at a local pet-industry meetup, and asks groomers at a pet-supply store. She does one practice interview with her cousin, a salon groomer, and does not count it.

**3. Interviews.** She asks how they book, what happened the last time a client did not show, and what they have tried. She does not mention her product.

**4. Patterns after 12 qualified interviews:**
- **Problem confirmed, but sharper than expected.** Salon groomers mentioned no-shows as an annoyance. **Mobile groomers** described them as their worst week-to-week problem, because a no-show also burns drive time and an empty slot they cannot backfill.
- **Behavior, not just talk.** 5 of 6 mobile groomers had already **tried** something: deposit requests by payment app, reminder texts sent by hand, or a strict cancellation policy. 4 were already **paying** $25–$45/month for generic booking software that did not handle deposits well.
- **Conditions to switch.** Every mobile groomer said they would switch only if they could import their existing client list and the tool took deposits automatically.
- **Answers became repetitive** by interview 10 within the mobile sub-group. She keeps interviewing salon groomers separately because that group is giving mixed answers.

**5. Market check.** She lists competitors and alternatives: generic booking apps, payment-app deposits, and paper calendars. She estimates how many mobile groomers are reachable through the groups and trade events she found. That data supports her interview evidence. It does not stand in for it.

**6. Solution last.** Only now does she show a clickable mockup. **Desirable:** groomers asked when they could start. Two offered to pay the first month up front. **Feasible:** deposits plus a client import are buildable by her and one contractor. **Viable:** groomers already pay $25–$45 for weaker tools, so $29 fits.

**7. Change and adapt.** She narrows her segment from "independent groomers" to **mobile groomers**, moves client import to the top of the build list, and drops a social-media feature nobody asked about.

**Why this works:**
- She listed the "I think" assumptions first, each with a disproof condition.
- She talked to people who match the segment and profile, and kept family feedback out of the count.
- She found **which segment feels the problem most intensely** rather than assuming.
- She weighted **done / paid for / tried** evidence over stated interest.
- She captured the **conditions under which they would change behavior.**
- She tested the **specific price she needs**, anchored to what they already pay.
- She validated the **solution last**, with all three checks (Desirable / Feasible / Viable).
- She **changed the segment and the solution** based on evidence instead of defending her plan.

### Negative Example: The Campus Grocery-List App

*Illustrative example. All names and numbers are hypothetical.*

**Situation.** Jordan spends four months building a shared grocery-list app for college roommates. Then he "validates" it.

- He shows the finished app to his three roommates, his girlfriend, and his parents. Everyone says they love it.
- He posts a poll in a general campus group: "Would you use an app that splits grocery lists with roommates?" 41 of 50 vote yes. He calls this "82% demand."
- He cites an industry report on the size of the online grocery market as proof that the opportunity is huge.
- Three students mention that they already use a shared notes app or a group chat for this. He decides they "just haven't seen a good enough app yet."
- He never asks about price. He plans to charge $3.99/month "later."
- He launches. Downloads stall after his friends install it, and almost no one comes back after the first week.

**Why this fails:**
- **Built first, then searched for a market.** Four months of product came before any evidence, which is exactly what validating early prevents.
- **Friends and family as evidence.** Roommates, a girlfriend, and parents are the Tom Fishburne cartoon in real life.
- **Statements of interest treated as proof.** A yes on a poll is what people say they might do. Nobody had done, paid for, or tried anything that suggested they would switch.
- **Assumed the problem looks the same to them as to him.** For most students, a shared note or group chat already solves it well enough. He never established that the real problem was painful.
- **Assumed he knew the segment.** "College students" was never narrowed to the group that feels the problem most often or most intensely, if any does.
- **Market data replaced customer evidence.** The size of the grocery market says nothing about whether roommates need this app.
- **Ignored competitors and alternatives.** The notes app and the group chat were the competition, and he dismissed them.
- **Afraid to change the solution.** He explained away the disconfirming evidence instead of adapting.
- **Never tested the price he needed.** Viability was never checked.
- **Product-led instead of need-led.** Every conversation was about his app, not about whether a need existed.
