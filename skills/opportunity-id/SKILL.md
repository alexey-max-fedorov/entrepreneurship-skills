# Opportunity Identification & Screening

You are an expert in entrepreneurial opportunity recognition and screening. Your goal is to help the user find **problems worth solving for profit**, understand those problems down to the root cause, and screen ideas with evidence so they put time and money only into real opportunities and drop weak ideas early.

## Before You Start

Gather this context first. If the user has already given it, don't ask again.

1. **Starting point.** Is there an idea or solution already, a complaint or problem, a change the user has noticed, or nothing yet?
2. **Who has the problem?** A specific person or group, not "everyone."
3. **Evidence so far.** Who has the user talked to? Strangers or friends and family? Any observations, data, or money spent?
4. **Current behavior.** What do these people do today? Workarounds, competitors, putting up with it, or nothing?
5. **Who pays?** Does the person who feels the pain have both the money and the motive to pay for a fix?
6. **Founder fit.** What skills, domain expertise, access, and resources does the user have?
7. **Goal of this session.** Is it finding opportunities, deepening one problem, screening one idea, comparing several, or auditing or pivoting an existing business?

If the user brings only a solution ("an app that…"), don't evaluate the solution first. Go back to the problem underneath it (see [Idea vs. Opportunity](#idea-vs-opportunity)).

## What an Opportunity Is

An **opportunity** is a potential market opening created by one of three things:

- **A customer problem**
- **A change** (social, economic, or regulatory)
- **An unmet need**

An opportunity is **not** "a cool product idea." It is a problem to investigate, not a business or a solution.

### The core shift: problem first, not product first

| Myth | Reality |
|---|---|
| An idea is a product or solution | Start with the **problem**, NOT the product or solution |
| My solution proves a need exists | A solution (idea) is **not proof** of a customer problem |
| Problem and solution are the same thing | **Problem ≠ Solution** |
| I can work out the problem later | Understanding the problem improves **every later decision** |

> **Fall in love with the problem, not your solution.**

### Problems worth solving for profit

This skill works as a **profit filter on problems.** The pain itself is covered in depth by [problem-statement](../problem-statement/SKILL.md). The question here is narrower: **can someone make money solving this problem?**

Many real problems still fail as businesses because:

- **The people who suffer it won't pay to fix it.** The pain is real but there's no willingness to pay, or the sufferer isn't the buyer. Map who pays vs. who suffers (→ [customer-ecosystem](../customer-ecosystem/SKILL.md)).
- **The people who suffer it are satisfied with their status quo.** Their current workaround is "good enough," so they won't switch.

A problem is **worth solving** when it is real, recurring, costly, actively worked around, and paid for. That is, when it clears the traits below **and** the [7 Criteria](#the-7-criteria-framework-screening).

### Traits of a real problem

The test for whether a problem is **real** is owned by → [problem-statement](../problem-statement/SKILL.md) (Real Problems: The Evidence Criteria). In short: people actually feel it, it's frequent, it's costly, and they already pay for it in some currency (money, time, effort, or a compromise they accept, called a **workaround**). A **preference is not a problem**. This skill takes a problem that has already passed that test and asks whether it's worth solving **for profit**.

## Idea vs. Opportunity

**Ideas are cheap.** An idea is a thought, a spark, or an intuition. There are many of them, but no validation, and chasing the wrong one carries a **high opportunity cost**. **Opportunities are validated ideas.**

| IDEA | OPPORTUNITY |
|---|---|
| "Wouldn't it be cool if…" | "People are actively searching for…" |
| Based on opinion/assumption | Validated by evidence |
| Technology- or feature-focused | Customer problem-focused |
| Unclear how to make money | Clear path to revenue |

### Why most ideas die

1. **No real problem**
2. **No market**
3. **No urgency**
4. **No willingness to pay**

### What separates ideas from opportunities

- **Identifiable customer**
- **Observable pain**
- **Willingness to pay**
- **Evidence vs. belief** (the deciding factor)

### When an idea becomes an opportunity

An idea becomes an opportunity **only after** it is:

1. **Refined around a real problem**
2. **Tested with evidence**
3. **Anchored to a specific customer** (→ [customer-segmentation](../customer-segmentation/SKILL.md))

Gut feeling isn't enough. You need a **systematic way to evaluate ideas**, which is what the [7 Criteria Framework](#the-7-criteria-framework-screening) provides.

### Quick classifier: idea (I) or opportunity (O)?

Statements built on the speaker's belief are **ideas**. Statements built on evidence from other people are **opportunity candidates** that still need screening.

| Statement | Call | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "A pen that can change ink colors on command… I bet people would like it" | **I** | Pure opinion ("I bet") |
| "Shoes that adapt to the weather… that seems logical, right?" | **I** | Logic, no evidence of demand |
| "Eco-friendly pet products: 78% of pet owners surveyed online are searching for them" | **O** | Evidence that people are actively searching |
| "Houston has been over 100 degrees 15 days in a row; people would probably use a portable instant drink chiller" | **I** | A real fact, but demand is assumed ("probably") |
| "Survey of gym-goers: 85% expressed interest in a wearable giving real-time posture feedback" | **O** | Evidence from the target users |
| "There should be an app that turns your screen into a mirror. I think most people would find it useful" | **I** | Opinion ("I think") |
| "Nutritious lunchbox snacks for children are expensive, at least that's what 20 moms at my son's school told me" | **O** | Pain heard from real users |
| "I've always believed there's a market for scented pencils" | **I** | Belief ("I've always believed") |

An "O" only means there's evidence worth screening. Stated interest is not payment, so run it through criterion 3 (Delivers Value) before treating it as proven.

## Sources of Opportunity: Where to Look

### The three high-value problem signals

| Signal | Look for | Example → problem |
|---|---|---|
| **Gatekeeping** | Who prevents access to something valuable? | Professional services (lawyers, doctors, designers) cost a lot → **lack of accessible/affordable expertise** |
| **Friction** | Things that require multiple steps, forms, or approvals | Mortgage applications: extensive documentation, repeated forms, multiple approvals → **the loan process is unnecessarily complex** |
| **Waste** | What's thrown away or underused? | Empty apartments in expensive cities → **underutilized assets** |

### Personal scan: five questions

| Question | Prompt |
|---|---|
| What frustrates me regularly? | What do I complain about? |
| What's inefficient in my daily life? | What could be automated? |
| What would I pay more for? | Quality, convenience, status |
| What do my friends/family/colleagues complain about? | What problems do they all mention? |
| What do I know that others don't? | What domain expertise do I have? |

Use these questions to **find** problems. Friends and family are fine for spotting a complaint, but they **don't count as validation** (see [Red Flags](#red-flags)).

### Change creates opportunity

The core chain:

```
Change / Shift  →  New Customer Problem  →  Entrepreneurial Response (Opportunity)
```

**1. Demographic & behavioral shifts.** Life changes create problems:

| Change | New problems |
|---|---|
| Aging | Mobility, health, technology |
| Parenthood | Time and financial constraints |
| Remote work | Isolation, collaboration |
| Moving | Social, navigation, services |

**2. Regulatory & government changes.** Regulation creates **immediate, urgent problems** for businesses and individuals because they are **legally required to comply** and can't ignore it. Regulation tends to create:

- Urgent compliance problems
- Clear deadlines
- New demand for expertise
- New demand for tools

Example prompt: what opportunities did NIL (college athlete name, image, and likeness rules) create?

**3. Crises.** Crises create opportunity by suddenly creating or sharpening customer problems.

Map changes with this template:

```
| Change / Shift | New Problem (who, what) | Opportunity (problem to investigate) |
|----------------|-------------------------|--------------------------------------|
|                |                         |                                      |
```

## Digging Into the Problem

Once you have a candidate problem, understand it before you think about solutions. The diagnostic tools are owned by → [problem-statement](../problem-statement/SKILL.md); run them there before screening:

| Tool | Question it answers | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **Current vs. Desired State gap** | What's the gap, and which of its components drives the pain? **The opportunity lies in the gap.** | problem-statement → The problem in entrepreneurial terms |
| **Listening for problems** | What do people complain about, put up with, or pay for partially? (Interview method → [customer-interview](../customer-interview/SKILL.md)) | problem-statement → Method 3: Observation & Listening |
| **Make it specific** | Can it be stated exactly, observed, and explained? | problem-statement → Real Problems: It is specific |
| **Workarounds** | What effort are people already spending? Workarounds expose real problems. | problem-statement → Method 4: Identify Workarounds |
| **Symptoms vs. root causes** | Is this the outcome, or a cause you can actually solve? | problem-statement → Symptoms, Causes, and Root Causes |
| **Laddering** | Why? Why? Why? Where's the root? | problem-statement → Method 2: Problem Laddering |
| **Jobs to Be Done** | What progress is the customer trying to make? | problem-statement → Method 1: Jobs-to-Be-Done |

The **Five Cs** below are owned here. They add the opportunity lens: when the customer cares enough to act, and what blocks or bends their behavior.

### The Five Cs of Opportunity Identification (Scott Anthony)

From Scott Anthony's article *The Five Cs of Opportunity Identification*. The Five Cs help you **understand the customer's situation before you propose a solution.**

| C | Meaning | Examples | Guiding question |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Circumstance** | The specific problem a customer cares about depends on their circumstance | A parent trying to diagnose an ear infection has a different health problem than a parent whose child broke an arm. Someone with a flat tire wants a different solution than someone planning what to do *if* they get a flat. | **When does this customer care enough to act?** |
| **Context** | Asking customers what they did in the past gives a loose picture of reality. Asking what they will do in the future gives an even murkier guess. The trick is to be with the customer when they hit the problem and watch how they solve it. Observation reveals details that surveys and predictions miss. | Be present at the moment of the problem | **What does the customer actually do in the moment?** |
| **Constraints** | A time-tested path to growth is innovating around a **barrier that limits consumption** | Southwest Airlines' discount service attracted people who would otherwise have taken the bus. The Wii appealed to families looking for simple entertainment. | **What prevents the customer from using an existing option?** |
| **Compensating Behaviors** | Using a product or service in an **unintended way** | Quicken being used for business (which led to QuickBooks). Bubble wrap was originally textured wallpaper. Play-Doh was originally wallpaper cleaner. Rogaine was originally a high blood pressure drug. | **What workaround, misuse, or improvised behavior reveals an unmet need?** |
| **Criteria** | What "good enough" means to this customer: **functional, emotional, social** | How the customer will judge the solution | **How will the customer judge whether the solution is worth choosing?** |

Fill-in template:

```
Circumstance:            When exactly does [customer] care enough to act? ...
Context:                 What did I OBSERVE them do in the moment? ...
Constraints:             What blocks them from existing options (cost, skill, access, time)? ...
Compensating behaviors:  What workaround / misuse / improvisation do they use? ...
Criteria:                Good enough = functional: ... | emotional: ... | social: ...
```

### Hand off to the problem statement

Once the problem is specific and the cause is understood, write it as a problem statement (who, what, when, why it matters, what they do now). The template, the common mistakes, and the problem statement test belong to → [problem-statement](../problem-statement/SKILL.md). A weak problem statement leads to a weak foundation, wasted effort on the wrong solution, and a higher risk of failure. A strong one gives clear direction, a stronger basis for product/market fit, and a better chance of success.

## The 7 Criteria Framework (Screening)

**Screening** (evaluation) is the step from generating ideas to evaluating them. The 7 Criteria Framework gives you a **systematic way to evaluate**, so you don't rely on gut feeling.

1. **Solves a Real Problem**
2. **Different/Better**
3. **Delivers Value** (people want it)
4. **Doable/Achievable**
5. **Acceptable Cost-Benefit** (worth the investment)
6. **Scalable**
7. **Defensible** (not easily copied)

> **Not every opportunity needs to score perfectly on all 7, but you should know where your strengths and weaknesses are.**

### 1. Solves a Real Problem

**Key question:** do people actually experience this pain?

- People feel the problem **regularly**
- It causes frustration, costs money, or creates inefficiency
- People **actively try to solve it** (with workarounds or competitors)
- People **would pay** to solve it

| Passes | Fails |
|---|---|
| Dropbox: people forgot USB drives and emailed files to themselves (**workaround = real problem**) | "People hate the color of coffee cups" (personal opinion, not a validated problem) |

### 2. Different/Better

**Key question:** why would someone switch to YOUR solution? (Differentiation is covered in → [value-proposition](../value-proposition/SKILL.md).)

- You improve on current solutions
- You're better on **at least one important dimension**: price, speed, quality, convenience
- There's a **clear reason customers would change behavior**

| Passes | Fails |
|---|---|
| Uber: more reliable, transparent pricing, easier than taxis (**10X better on convenience**) | A project management app with no clear advantage |

### 3. Delivers Value

**Key question:** will people actually PAY for this?

- People **WANT** your improvement (not just "that's cool")
- **Willingness to pay** exists
- **Value > Price** in the customer's mind (→ [pricing-models](../pricing-models/SKILL.md))

| Passes | Fails |
|---|---|
| Calm app: people pay $70/year because meditation reduces stress | A free app with no monetization plan |

### 4. Doable/Achievable

**Key question:** can YOU actually build and deliver this?

- Technically feasible with your skills (or skills you can hire)
- No impossible regulatory barriers
- You can get to market in reasonable time
- Supply chain and operations are realistic

| Passes | Fails |
|---|---|
| A software app you can code yourself or hire freelancers to build | Needing to invent new battery technology |

### 5. Acceptable Cost-Benefit

**Key question:** does the math actually work? (Revenue models and assessment are covered in → [business-model](../business-model/SKILL.md).)

- The investment (time, money, effort) is worth the potential return
- You can **acquire customers profitably**
- **Unit economics are positive** (gross profit per unit, break-even → [financials](../financials/SKILL.md))
- A **path to profitability** exists

| Passes | Fails |
|---|---|
| A SaaS app that costs $5/month to serve and charges $50/month (positive margin) | Spending $100 to acquire a customer for a one-time $30 sale |

### 6. Scalable

**Key question:** can this grow beyond your first customers?

- The market is large enough (sizing is covered in → [market-research](../market-research/SKILL.md))
- The business gets **EASIER as you grow, not harder**
- You can reach more customers **without a proportional cost increase**

| Passes | Fails |
|---|---|
| Software: new users are added at near-zero marginal cost | A personal consulting service where revenue is tied to your own time |

### 7. Defensible

**Key question:** what stops competitors from copying you?

- You have a **moat** (a competitive barrier)
- **First-mover advantage is NOT a moat**
- Something **structural** protects your position

**Common moats:**

| Moat | How it protects | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Patents / IP** | Legal protection | Velcro's patent |
| **Network effects** | More users = more value | Facebook, Uber |
| **Brand** | Customer loyalty | Nike, Apple |
| **Switching costs** | Hard to leave once the customer has invested | Salesforce |

| Passes | Fails |
|---|---|
| Airbnb's network effects (more hosts → more guests → more hosts) | "We will execute better than competitors" |

### Screening scorecard (template)

```
| Criterion              | Score (Yes / Partial / No / Unknown) | Why (evidence, not opinion) |
|------------------------|----------------------------|-----------------------------|
| 1. Solves Real Problem |                            |                             |
| 2. Different/Better    |                            |                             |
| 3. Delivers Value      |                            |                             |
| 4. Doable              |                            |                             |
| 5. Cost-Benefit        |                            |                             |
| 6. Scalable            |                            |                             |
| 7. Defensible          |                            |                             |
Weakest criteria: ...      Plan to test or fix them: ...
Decision: Pursue / Refine & re-screen / Abandon
```

Mark a criterion **Unknown** when you have no evidence for it yet. Unknown is a task to go test, not a pass.

### Reference: a 7/7 scorecard (Velcro)

George de Mestral got the idea from burrs stuck to his dog and copied the hook-and-loop mechanism. Founded 1941, patented 1955, started as a $60/week business, now $319M in revenue.

| Criterion | Score | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Solves Real Problem | Yes | Made fastening easier |
| Different/Better | Yes | Nothing like it on the market, entirely new |
| Delivers Value | Yes | Secures items, inexpensive |
| Doable | Yes | Could be manufactured |
| Cost-Benefit | Yes | Could sell for more than it cost to make |
| Scalable | Yes | Many uses, mass-manufactured |
| Defensible | Yes | Patent |

Founder stories also show where opportunities come from. Under Armour (Kevin Plank, University of Maryland football player) started from an observed pain: cotton T-shirts under pads were always soaked and heavy with sweat. It was founded in 1995 in his grandmother's basement on $40,000 of credit cards and now has $4.8B in revenue.

## What Separates Good from Great

- **Clear problem**
- **Clear user** (→ [customer-end-user-profile-and-persona](../customer-end-user-profile-and-persona/SKILL.md))
- **Non-obvious insight**
- **Execution matters** (Under Armour, Uber)

> **"Opportunities look obvious in hindsight."** If the insight already looks obvious to everyone, check whether you actually have one.

## Red Flags

| Red flag | What it really means |
|---|---|
| "Everyone I talked to loved it" | **Friends and family don't count** |
| "We have no competitors" | **You're probably wrong.** Ask why there are none (→ [market-research](../market-research/SKILL.md)) |
| "We just need 1% of the market" | **There's no go-to-market plan** |
| "We'll figure out revenue later" | **The revenue model has to be clear** (→ [business-model](../business-model/SKILL.md)) |
| Solving a problem that doesn't exist or isn't painful enough | Fails criterion 1 |

## Workflow

Use this workflow whether the user is starting fresh, auditing an existing business, revising, or pivoting.

1. **Locate the starting point.** If you have a solution, extract the problem underneath it. If you have a complaint, it's already a problem candidate. If you have nothing, run the source scan.
2. **Scan sources.** Use the three signals (Gatekeeping, Friction, Waste), the five personal-scan questions, and the change map (demographic and behavioral shifts, regulation, crises). List the candidate problems.
3. **Apply the preference vs. problem filter.** Keep only candidates that are real, frequent, costly, and already being worked around.
4. **Apply the profit filter.** For each survivor, ask whether the people who feel it will pay, and whether they're satisfied with their status quo. Drop candidates where nobody pays or the workaround is good enough.
5. **Dig in.** Map the Current vs. Desired gap, listen for complaints and workarounds, ladder to the root cause, name the job to be done, and fill in the Five Cs. Prefer **observation** over asking people to predict.
6. **Write the problem statement.** Hand off to [problem-statement](../problem-statement/SKILL.md) and run its test.
7. **Check idea vs. opportunity.** Is there an identifiable customer, observable pain, willingness to pay, and evidence rather than belief? Is the idea refined around a real problem, tested with evidence, and anchored to a specific customer?
8. **Screen with the 7 Criteria.** Fill in the scorecard with evidence. Mark unknowns.
9. **Check the red flags.** Any one present means the work isn't done.
10. **Decide.** Pursue (move on to validation, value proposition, and business model), refine and re-screen (fix the weakest criteria), or **abandon early.** Screening saves time, and it's better to abandon bad ideas early.

## Common Mistakes

- **Starting from the product.** "Wouldn't it be cool if…" is an idea, not an opportunity. Go back to the problem.
- **Treating a solution as proof of a problem.** A clever solution proves nothing about demand.
- **Confusing preferences with problems.** "I wish it came in my color" isn't a pain that people pay to fix.
- **Ignoring who pays.** Real pain that the sufferer won't or can't pay to remove isn't a profitable opportunity.
- **Underestimating the status quo.** If the current workaround is good enough, people won't switch.
- **Stopping at the symptom.** "Sales are down" is an outcome. Ladder until you reach a cause you can act on.
- **Relying on predictions.** Asking what people *will* do gives a murky guess. Watch what they actually do in the moment.
- **Counting friends and family as validation.** Evidence must come from real customers. Evidence beats opinion.
- **Mistaking stated interest for payment.** "85% expressed interest" makes something an opportunity candidate, but criterion 3 asks whether they will **pay.**
- **Claiming first-mover advantage as a moat.** It isn't one. Name a structural barrier.
- **"We'll execute better."** That's not defensibility.
- **Scaling with headcount.** If revenue rises only with your hours, the business doesn't scale.
- **Copying novelty or fad hits.** Some novelty products have succeeded on exceptional salesmanship without solving a real problem. They don't follow the usual playbook, and most copiers fail. Don't model a venture on them.
- **Demanding a perfect 7/7, or ignoring weak spots.** Not every criterion needs to be perfect, but you must know your weaknesses and have a plan for them. (That tolerance applies to the *opportunity*, not to your screening. Always aim for perfection in the work itself: score every criterion, cite the evidence, and name every weak spot.)

## Worked Examples

### Positive: make-ready coordination for mid-size apartment operators

**Starting point.** A founder who worked two summers as a leasing assistant at a 240-unit Houston complex noticed a recurring **waste** signal: units sat empty for days after move-out while the manager chased the cleaner, painter, and maintenance vendor by text.

**Digging in.**

- **Current vs. desired.** Today, a turned unit takes 9–12 days from move-out to rent-ready. The desired state is a rent-ready unit within 5 days, without the manager acting as dispatcher.
- **Listening (14 interviews with property managers at complexes of 150–400 units, none of them friends).** "I keep paying for rush cleaning." "I've just accepted that turns take two weeks." Workaround: a shared spreadsheet plus group texts plus walking each unit.
- **Context.** The founder observed 2 make-readys in person and saw a painter arrive before the cleaner had finished. That's a sequencing failure surveys would have missed.
- **Laddering.** Slow turns ← vendors arrive out of order ← no one sees real-time status ← status lives in the manager's head and texts. **Root cause:** there's no shared, sequenced view of turn tasks across outside vendors.
- **Five Cs.** Circumstance: peak move-out months, when several turns overlap. Constraints: enterprise property software is priced and built for large portfolios. Compensating behavior: managers repurposing spreadsheets and group texts as a scheduling system. Criteria: functional (fewer vacant days), emotional (stop chasing vendors), social (looking competent to the owner).

**Problem statement** (built with problem-statement): "Property managers at independent 150–400-unit complexes lose 4–7 rentable days per turned unit during move-out because cleaning, paint, and repair vendors are scheduled by text with no shared sequence. Each vacant day costs a day's rent. They cope with spreadsheets, rush fees, and walking units daily."

**Screening.**

| Criterion | Score | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Solves Real Problem | Yes | 12 of 14 managers described the same workaround. Rush fees are money already spent. |
| Different/Better | Yes | Better on speed and convenience than spreadsheets and texts, and lighter than enterprise suites |
| Delivers Value | Yes | Vacant days have a direct dollar cost, so value > price is easy to show. 5 managers agreed to a paid pilot. |
| Doable | Yes | Software the founder can build with one freelancer, no regulatory barrier |
| Cost-Benefit | Yes (to confirm) | Planned per-unit monthly subscription with low serving cost. Customer acquisition cost is still to be measured in the pilot. |
| Scalable | Yes | Software, and new properties add near-zero marginal cost |
| Defensible | Partial | Switching costs once vendor networks and turn history live in the tool. No patent, and first-mover is not counted as a moat. |

**Why it's strong:** it started from an observed waste signal, not a product. The evidence came from strangers and direct observation. The root cause is specific and solvable. The payer (the manager or owner) is the one losing money. Weaknesses are named, not hidden: Defensible is partial and CAC is unproven, and each has a test planned. Next steps are validation, the value proposition, and the business model.

### Negative: an AI outfit-recommendation app

**Pitch.** "An app that uses AI to suggest outfits based on the weather and your calendar. Everyone I showed it to loved it. There are no real competitors, and we just need 1% of the fashion market. It'll be free at launch, and we'll figure out revenue later."

**Screening.**

| Criterion | Score | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Solves Real Problem | No | Starts from a technology ("wouldn't it be cool if"). No evidence of frequent, costly pain. Choosing an outfit is a preference, not a paid-for problem. |
| Different/Better | Unknown | No dimension (price, speed, quality, convenience) on which it beats the status quo of checking the weather and picking clothes |
| Delivers Value | No | Free with no monetization plan. "That's cool" isn't willingness to pay. |
| Doable | Yes | Buildable software |
| Cost-Benefit | No | Revenue is undefined, so unit economics can't be positive |
| Scalable | Unknown | Software scales, but there is no proven market to scale into |
| Defensible | No | No moat. Any weather or fashion app can copy the feature. |

**Red flags hit:** all four verbal ones. "Everyone loved it" (friends and family), "no competitors" (wrong, since weather apps, shopping apps, and the status quo all compete), "1% of the market" (no go-to-market plan), and "revenue later." The fifth applies too: the problem isn't painful enough.

**Why it fails:** it's an **idea**, not an opportunity. There's no specific customer, no observable pain, no willingness to pay, and only belief rather than evidence. **The fix:** drop the solution, go back to the source scan, and look for a real problem with a buyer (for example, listen to people with strict workplace dress codes or to stylists' clients) before building anything. Or abandon it early, which is the cheap outcome screening is designed to produce.
