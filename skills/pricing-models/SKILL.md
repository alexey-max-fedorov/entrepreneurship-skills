## Before You Start

Pricing can be done from scratch (launch), as an audit (is the current price right?), as a re-price (raise, lower, restructure), or as a model change (for example, moving from cost-plus to value-based). Gather the following before recommending anything. If something is missing, say so and either ask, estimate it with a stated assumption, or make it the "evidence still needed" in the recommendation.

1. **What exactly is being priced?** Name the product or service precisely. If you are asked to charge for picking up someone's Starbucks order, **you are pricing the pickup service, not the coffee.**
2. **Who is the customer?** The specific segment, and who actually pays (→ [customer-segmentation](../customer-segmentation/SKILL.md), [customer-ecosystem](../customer-ecosystem/SKILL.md)).
3. **What alternatives does the customer see**, and what do they cost? Include doing it themselves and doing nothing (→ [market-research](../market-research/SKILL.md)).
4. **What does one unit cost** to produce and deliver? Materials, labor, and overhead per unit (→ [financials](../financials/SKILL.md)).
5. **What is the business trying to achieve with price?** Maximum profit, fast market share, recovering R&D, growing order size, signaling luxury.
6. **What stage and market conditions?** New-to-market innovation, market entry against incumbents, stable established market, commodity market, volatile demand.
7. **Any existing price and data?** Current price, sales volume, conversion, past price changes and what happened.
8. **Constraints?** Regulatory considerations, contracts, channel partners, published price lists.
9. **Your opening answer.** Write down your gut price now, before analysis. The final step asks what changed.

## Price, Revenue, and Profit: The Basics

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Price** | What a customer pays. |
| **Revenue** | Price × units sold. |
| **Profit** | Revenue − total costs. |

- **A higher price does not automatically mean a higher profit.** Price changes how many units you sell; costs decide what is left over.
- Value and price fit together like puzzle pieces: the price has to match the value the customer perceives (→ [value-proposition](../value-proposition/SKILL.md)).
- Revenue, profit, COGS, and the full financial statements are owned by → [financials](../financials/SKILL.md). This skill uses them only to judge a price.

### Three Questions Every Pricer Must Answer

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| What pushes market prices up or down? | Shifts in **supply and demand**. |
| What determines customer price sensitivity? | **Elasticity**. |
| Why does more revenue not always mean more profit? | **Costs decide profit.** |

### Why People Pay Different Prices for the Same Thing

The same product (coffee) sells at wildly different prices (illustrative prices from the deck):

| Coffee | Price (illustrative) |
|---|---|
| Home-brewed pot | ~$0.36 per cup |
| Keurig pod | ~$0.75 |
| 7-Eleven regular | $2.23 |
| Starbucks grande latte | $3.65+ |

Identical products: so **what else are customers buying?** **Convenience, preparation, location, experience, brand.** When you price, you are pricing the whole bundle of these, not just the physical item. This is why "what exactly is being priced?" is the first question.

## What Determines Price

**You (the business) set the asking price(s).**

- **Customers decide** whether the offer is worth buying.
- **Costs, competitors, and market conditions** dictate the choice.
- **Customers vote with their feet: every purchase is a vote for your price.** Every non-purchase is a vote against it.

### The 8 Factors That Impact Price

| Factor | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| **Production costs** | What does one unit cost to make and deliver? |
| **Competition** | What do the alternatives charge, and how do customers compare them? |
| **Customer demand** | How many customers want this, at which prices, in a given period? |
| **Perceived value** | What is it worth to this customer? |
| **Market trends** | Is the market moving toward or away from this kind of offer? |
| **Economic conditions** | Are costs, inflation, and customer incomes rising or falling? |
| **Brand positioning** | What does our price say about us? |
| **Regulatory considerations** | Do any rules cap, set, or add cost to the price? |

### The Three Questions That Matter Most

Of the eight factors, three matter most:

1. **What does it cost to produce?** (production costs → the floor; → [financials](../financials/SKILL.md))
2. **What are the alternatives (competition)?** (the reference the customer compares against; → [market-research](../market-research/SKILL.md))
3. **What is it worth to this customer (perceived value)?** (the ceiling; → [value-proposition](../value-proposition/SKILL.md))

A workable price sits above cost, is defensible against the alternatives, and is at or below perceived value.

### What Is the "Right Price"? Three Views

| View | The right price is… |
|---|---|
| **Customer** | A price that seems good compared to the other options available. |
| **Business** | The price that maximizes profit or helps the company hit its goals. |
| **Economics** | The **equilibrium or clearing price**, where quantity supplied equals quantity demanded. |

**Note: a market-clearing price is not automatically the price that maximizes your profit.** A strong recommendation addresses all three views at once.

## Supply and Demand

### Definitions

- **Supply:** the quantities producers are willing and able to offer at different prices. The supply curve slopes **up**: higher prices bring more supply.
- **Demand:** the quantities customers are willing and able to buy (pay for) at different prices **during a specified period**. The demand curve slopes **down**: higher prices bring fewer buyers.
- **Equilibrium:** the point where the supply curve and demand curve cross. The price there is the **equilibrium price** (clearing price); the quantity is the **equilibrium quantity**.

### What the Model Assumes

The supply-demand-price equation assumes:

- A **free marketplace**
- **Willing buyers**
- **Willing sellers**

If one of these is missing (a regulated price, a monopoly supplier, captive buyers), the model's predictions weaken. Check regulatory considerations from the 8 factors.

### The Four Price Implications

Use these to predict the direction of price when the market shifts:

| Situation | Price direction |
|---|---|
| Supply held static, **demand increases** | **Prices rise** |
| Supply held static, **demand falls** | **Prices fall** |
| Demand held static, **supply increases** | **Prices fall** |
| Demand held static, **supply decreases** | **Prices rise** |

### Real World: The 2020–2022 Supply Shock

- **What happened:** COVID broke supply chains globally: chips, containers, labor, fuel.
- **Supply:** decreased significantly.
- **Demand:** stayed steady or rose.
- **Model says:** prices will rise.
- **Did it happen?** Yes: U.S. inflation hit **8.0% in 2022**.

### Inflation vs. the Price Level (Dated Figures)

These figures are dated. Keep the year attached when you cite them and refresh them before using them in a live decision.

| Year | U.S. CPI inflation (annual average) |
|---|---|
| 2021 | 4.7% |
| 2022 | 8.0% (peak) |
| 2023 | 4.1% |
| 2024 | 2.9% |
| 2025 | 2.6% |

- **2026 broke the trend:** September 2026 CPI was **3.7% year over year** (BLS, released October 14, 2026), with **gasoline up 34%** on the energy shock.
- **Inflation is the rate prices rise; the price level is where prices are.** Lower inflation never meant prices fell, only that they rose more slowly. As of 2026, **the price level is still ~20% above 2021.**
- **Why it matters for pricing:** a price set in 2021 and never revisited buys about 17% less by 2026 (1 − 1/1.20), while input costs (and customer reference prices) moved up. When re-pricing, check how your unit costs and the alternatives' prices have moved since your last price change.

## Elasticity: How Sensitive Customers Are to Price

**How do customers respond to price changes?** Differently across products and services, because they face different numbers of alternatives.

| Type | Definition | Typical when | Deck example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Elastic demand** | Quantity demanded changes by a **larger** percentage than price. A small change in price leads to a large change in quantity demanded. | There are **many alternatives**. | **Cola:** Cola A raises its price 15%; quantity demanded falls hard as buyers switch to Cola B. |
| **Inelastic demand** | Quantity demanded changes by a **smaller** percentage than price. Price rises significantly; quantity demanded changes a little. | There are **few substitutes**. | **Gasoline:** the price doubles and quantity demanded falls only slightly; relatively inelastic **in the short run**. |

**Before you change your price, ask: "How much more or less will customers buy if I change my price?"** That is why elasticity matters.

### What a Price Change Does

| Price change | Quantity response | Revenue implication | Gross profit note |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Lower price** | Quantity demanded rises | **Rises if elastic; falls if inelastic** | Gross profit per unit falls |
| **Higher price** | Quantity demanded falls | **Rises if inelastic; falls if elastic** | Gross profit per unit rises |

**Gross profit per unit = price − unit COGS ($).** COGS and gross profit are owned by → [financials](../financials/SKILL.md).

### Worked Math: Revenue vs. Profit (Hypothetical Numbers from the Deck)

At $9 you sell 115 units. At $10 you sell 100.

**Revenue:**
- $9 × 115 = **$1,035**
- $10 × 100 = **$1,000**
- **Lower price wins on revenue.** (Price rose 11.1%, quantity fell 13.0%: quantity moved by a larger percentage, so this demand is elastic and the price increase lowered revenue, exactly as the table predicts.)

**Profit (if COGS is $8 per unit):**
- 115 × $8 = $920; $1,035 − $920 = **$115**
- 100 × $8 = $800; $1,000 − $800 = **$200**
- **Higher price wins on profit.**

**Elasticity tells you about revenue. Costs determine profit.** Never pick a price on revenue alone.

## The Nine Pricing Models

### What This Library Means by "Pricing Model"

In this library, the approaches below are called **pricing models**: the method a business uses to set and move its price. **A business can apply one pricing model or several at the same time** (see [Combining Pricing Models](#combining-pricing-models)).

A pricing model is not the same as a **revenue model** or business model type (how the business makes money: sales, subscription, freemium, razor and blade, and so on), which is owned by → [business-model](../business-model/SKILL.md). The revenue model decides *what* you charge for; the pricing model decides *how much* and *how the number moves*.

### Summary Comparison Table

| Pricing model | What it is | Best for | Use when | Biggest risk | Time frame |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Cost-Plus** | Markup over unit cost | Stable markets | Stable, established markets | Ignores what customers will pay: money left on the table, or priced out | Ongoing |
| **Value-Based** | Price on customer value | Differentiated products | Products are differentiated; branding and innovation matter | Misreading perceived value and overpricing | Long-term |
| **Competitive** | Price against rivals | Commodity markets | Commodity markets, highly competitive industries | Price wars that strip everyone's margin | Ongoing |
| **Penetration** | Start low, raise later | Market entry | New market entry; volume and traction matter most | Customers anchor on the low price and resist every increase | Launch / market-entry |
| **Skimming** | Start high, lower over time | Innovation, first-to-market | Innovative, first-to-market products | The high price advertises the market to competitors | Declining |
| **Prestige** | Price signals quality | Luxury and premium | Luxury positioning, where the brand is key | The experience fails to support the price, and the signal collapses | Long-term |
| **Dynamic** | Real-time price changes | Volatile demand | Demand is volatile and you can adjust quickly | Customers feel gouged and lose trust | Real-time |
| **Freemium** | Free base, paid upgrades | Digital, network effects | Digital products where network effects matter | Free users cost money but never convert | Ongoing |
| **Bundle** | Package deal | Growing order size | Complementary products that naturally belong together | Discounting sales you would have made at full price | Ongoing |

### Cost-Plus Pricing

- **Definition:** add all costs plus a markup for profit.
- **How it works (formula):** **(Materials + Labor + Overhead) per unit × (1 + Markup %) = Price**
- **Use when:** stable, established markets.
- **Best for:** stable markets.
- **Biggest risk:** it ignores what customers will pay. You can **leave money on the table, or price yourself out**.
- **Time frame:** Ongoing.

**Example (the deck's cost-plus illustration, re-told with realistic numbers).** A small-batch candle maker:

| Component (per candle) | Cost |
|---|---|
| Materials (wax, wick, fragrance, jar) | $6.50 |
| Labor (pouring, curing, labeling) | $4.00 |
| Overhead (studio rent, utilities, allocated per unit) | $1.50 |
| **Unit cost** | **$12.00** |
| Markup 50% | +$6.00 |
| **Price** | **$18.00** |

**Clean, defensible, and built without asking a single customer anything.** That last part is the model's weakness: if comparable hand-poured candles sell at $28, the maker just gave away $10 a candle; if they sell at $14, nobody buys at $18.

**Advantages**
- Simple to calculate
- Adds a markup to the estimated unit cost; does not guarantee overall profit
- Stable pricing
- Transparent

**Disadvantages**
- Ignores market conditions
- Ignores customer value
- Limited flexibility
- May ignore efficiency

#### Markup vs. Margin: 50% Markup Is Not 50% Margin

Using the candle: cost $12, 50% markup, price $18. There is a **$6 difference, but $6 out of what?**

| Measure | Formula | Candle | Measured on |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Markup** | (Price − Cost) ÷ **Cost** | $6 ÷ $12 = **50%** | **Cost** |
| **Margin** | (Price − Cost) ÷ **Price** | $6 ÷ $18 = **33.3%** | **Price** |

The same dollar difference is always a smaller percentage of price than of cost, so **margin is always lower than markup**. Convert between them:

- Margin = Markup ÷ (1 + Markup)
- Markup = Margin ÷ (1 − Margin)

| Markup | Equivalent margin |
|---|---|
| 25% | 20.0% |
| 50% | 33.3% |
| 100% | 50.0% |
| 150% | 60.0% |
| 200% | 66.7% |

If a target is stated as a margin ("we need 50%"), you need a **100% markup**, not 50%. Gross margin % on the income statement is owned by → [financials](../financials/SKILL.md).

### Value-Based Pricing

- **Definition:** start with customer value and willingness to pay, then check that the economics work.
- **How it works:** evaluate what customers will pay based on the benefits they get (→ [value-proposition](../value-proposition/SKILL.md)).
- **Use when:** products are differentiated; branding and innovation matter.
- **Best for:** differentiated products.
- **Biggest risk:** you misread perceived value and overprice.
- **Time frame:** Long-term.
- **This is where money is made: capturing value, not just recovering costs.**

**Slide example: Apple.** An iPhone sells for **$999 and up** (as cited in the deck). The physical components are only part of that price. **What are customers paying for beyond the physical components?**
- Brand
- Design
- Ecosystem
- Status
- Resale value
- The fact that everything just works together

Willingness to pay must be tested with the person who pays, not assumed (→ [customer-validation](../customer-validation/SKILL.md)).

**Advantages**
- Higher profit margins
- Customer-centric
- Competitive differentiation
- Encourages innovation

**Disadvantages**
- Complex to implement
- Market sensitive
- Risk of overpricing
- Time-intensive

### Competitive Pricing

- **Definition:** set price based on what competitors charge.
- **How it works:** evaluate competitor prices and align with, below, or above them (→ [market-research](../market-research/SKILL.md)).
- **Use when:** commodity markets, highly competitive industries.
- **Best for:** commodity markets.
- **Biggest risk:** price wars that strip everyone's margin.
- **Time frame:** Ongoing.

**Your price sends a signal:**
- A lot **higher** than rivals raises a **value question**.
- A lot **lower** than rivals raises a **quality question**.

**Slide example: streaming music.** Assume $9.99/month is the market standard.
- Launch at **$11.99**: customers hesitate: too expensive, is it worth it?
- Launch at **$7.99**: customers wonder: is it low quality?
- Launch at **$9.99**: customers think: same as the others.

**The key question:** at $11.99, what would we need to offer that makes the extra money worthwhile? (That answer is a differentiation question → [value-proposition](../value-proposition/SKILL.md).)

**Advantages**
- Simple to implement
- Market alignment
- Lower customer loss risk
- Good in saturated markets

**Disadvantages**
- Narrow profit margins
- No differentiation
- Ignores customer perception
- Risk of price wars

### Penetration Pricing

- **Definition:** a low price at launch to gain market share quickly, with increases possible later.
- **How it works:** enter with volume, build the customer base, then adjust price.
- **Use when:** new market entry, when volume and gaining traction matter most.
- **Best for:** market entry.
- **Biggest risk:** customers **anchor on the low price** and resist every increase.
- **Time frame:** Launch / market-entry.
- **Link to supply and demand:** a change in income can shift demand, and a change in price can shift demand. Plan for how both your later price increase and your customers' budgets will move the quantity you sell.

**Slide example: streaming service.**
- Launches at **$7.99** against a **$9.99** market.
- Price attracts customers; the base grows: **1M, 3M, 5M**.
- Once the customer base grows, raise the price to **$10.99**.
- **Ask: Will customers stay after the increase? What would make them leave?** (Answer these with interviews before the increase → [customer-interview](../customer-interview/SKILL.md).)

**Advantages**
- Rapid market entry
- Builds market share
- Lower production costs at scale
- High turnover

**Disadvantages**
- Profit pressure: thin margins
- Customer loyalty risk
- Perception of low quality
- Requires scale to work

### Skimming Pricing

- **Definition:** a high price at launch, lowered over time as competition enters.
- **How it works:** charge a premium to early adopters, then lower the price for the mass market.
- **Use when:** innovative, first-to-market products.
- **Best for:** innovation, first-to-market.
- **Biggest risk:** the high price **advertises the market to competitors**.
- **Time frame:** Declining.
- **The opposite of penetration:** penetration starts low and goes high; skimming starts high and goes low.

**Slide example: PlayStation 3.**
- **2006 launch: $599**: early adopters pay the premium.
- **2010: $299**: the price reaches the next layer of buyers.
- **Late life: around $199**: the mass market layer.
- The same pattern opened the flat-screen era: early **plasma TVs launched at $10,000 and up in the late 1990s**.

**Advantages**
- Maximizes early profits
- Recovers R&D costs
- Signals high quality
- Room to adjust down

**Disadvantages**
- Limited initial market
- Attracts competitors
- Customer dissatisfaction
- Bad for price-sensitive markets

### Prestige Pricing

- **Definition:** a higher price signals quality and exclusivity.
- **How it works:** the price is set deliberately high so that the price itself communicates quality and exclusivity; the brand and the experience must back it up. **Price itself becomes part of the value proposition** (→ [value-proposition](../value-proposition/SKILL.md)).
- **Use when:** luxury positioning, where the brand is key.
- **Best for:** luxury and premium.
- **Biggest risk:** the experience fails to support the price, and **the signal collapses**.
- **Time frame:** Long-term.
- **A higher price can signal quality and exclusivity. The brand and experience must support it.**

**Slide examples:**
- **Supreme hoodie:** a plain hoodie carries a premium many times a fast-fashion price: you are buying the brand.
- **StanCraft boats:** premium wooden lake boats priced far above production boats: craftsmanship and image are the product.
- **Target customer:** wealthy, image matters, wants the best (→ [customer-segmentation](../customer-segmentation/SKILL.md)).

**Advantages**
- Entry barrier: high marketing cost deters rivals
- High profit margins
- Brand loyalty
- Premium positioning

**Disadvantages**
- Massive branding cost
- Must maintain prestige
- Limited sales volume
- Competitors attacking

### Dynamic Pricing

- **Definition:** prices fluctuate based on real-time supply and demand.
- **How it works:** an algorithm adjusts automatically as market conditions change.
- **Use when:** demand is volatile and you can adjust quickly.
- **Best for:** volatile demand.
- **Biggest risk:** customers feel gouged and lose trust in you.
- **Time frame:** Real-time.
- **Link to supply and demand:** supply down, price up. **Dynamic pricing automates the supply and demand model in real time.**

**Slide examples:**
- **Airlines:** as seats fill, fares climb: early seats might go at **$150**, the last ones at **$400**.
- **Uber:** normal demand is **1x**; a rainy Friday night price can surge to **4x**.
- **The key question:** when does a changing price feel reasonable, and when does it feel unfair? Answer it for your customers before launching dynamic pricing, because perceived unfairness is a listed disadvantage.

**Advantages**
- Maximizes revenue
- Real-time flexibility
- Optimizes inventory
- Customer segmentation

**Disadvantages**
- Customer dissatisfaction
- Technology cost
- Perceived unfairness
- Risk of price wars

### Freemium Pricing

- **Definition:** offer the basic service free, charge for premium features.
- **How it works:** **the free tier proves the value; the paid tier removes the friction.** You set where the free/paid line sits and what the paid tier costs.
- **Use when:** digital products, where network effects matter.
- **Best for:** digital, network effects.
- **Goal:** build a massive user base and convert a share of it to paying.
- **Biggest risk:** free users cost (you) money but they never convert.
- **Time frame:** Ongoing.
- **The key question: what makes someone upgrade?**

**Slide examples:**
- **Dropbox:** the free tier is 2GB of storage; paid tiers start at 2TB.
- **Duolingo:** free lessons carry ads; paid removes ads and adds offline access.

**Advantages**
- Builds a massive user base quickly
- Free users market the product for you
- Network effects compound
- Low-friction trial proves the value

**Disadvantages**
- Most users never pay
- Free users cost real money to serve
- Conversion is never guaranteed
- The premium tier must be clearly worth it

#### Freemium as a Pricing Model vs. a Revenue Model

Freemium also appears in [business-model](../business-model/SKILL.md) as a **revenue model** (how the business makes money: basic services free, charge a premium for add-ons); here it is a **pricing model** (how you set the price: where the free line sits, what the paid tier costs, and what triggers the upgrade). For whether freemium fits the overall business, go to → [business-model](../business-model/SKILL.md).

### Bundle Pricing

- **Definition:** sell multiple products together at a discounted price. A bundle:
  - Increases average order value
  - Moves slower-moving items alongside fast sellers
  - Creates perceived value for customers
- **How it works:** determine the products that go together, and bundle them.
- **Use when:** complementary products that naturally belong together.
- **Best for:** growing order size.
- **Biggest risk:** discounting sales you would have made at full price.
- **Time frame:** Ongoing.
- **The key question: does the bundle increase profit, or simply discount purchases customers would have made anyway?**

**Slide example: McDonald's Value Meal.** Burger + fries + drink, priced below the three items bought separately.
- The customer feels a deal, and orders all three, every time.
- McDonald's sells fries and a drink on every single ticket.

**Advantages**
- Increases average order value
- Moves slow sellers alongside fast sellers
- Creates perceived value
- Simplifies the customer's decision

**Disadvantages**
- Can discount sales customers would have made anyway
- Margin give-away on full-price items
- Can complicate the offer
- Ties up inventory in packages

### Penetration vs. Skimming at a Glance

| | Penetration | Skimming |
|---|---|---|
| Launch price | Low | High |
| Direction | Raise later | Lower over time |
| First buyers | Price-sensitive volume | Early adopters who pay a premium |
| Wins | Market share, traction, scale | Early profit, R&D recovery |
| Classic failure | Customers anchor low and resist increases | High price invites competitors |
| Bad fit | Products that cannot reach scale | Price-sensitive markets |

## Combining Pricing Models

**A business can run one pricing model or several at the same time.** Most real prices are combinations: one model sets the number, another decides how it moves, another shapes the package.

### How the Nine Models Stack

The models answer different questions and run on different clocks (see the time-frame column: ongoing, long-term, launch, declining, real-time), which is why they can be layered. This skill groups them by the decision each one makes:

| Decision | Models that make it |
|---|---|
| **Where does the number come from?** | Cost-Plus (from cost), Value-Based (from customer value), Competitive (from rivals) |
| **How does the price move over time?** | Penetration (low → higher), Skimming (high → lower), Dynamic (real-time) |
| **What does the price signal?** | Prestige |
| **How is the offer packaged?** | Freemium (free base + paid upgrades), Bundle (package deal), plus good-better-best tiers from pricing psychology |

### Rules for Combining

1. **Name one primary pricing model.** The recommendation checklist asks for "a primary pricing approach." The others are supporting layers, each with a named job.
2. **Triangulate the number with all three key questions.** Use cost (cost-plus logic) as the **floor**, perceived value (value-based logic) as the **ceiling**, and competitor prices (competitive logic) as the **reference** the customer will compare against. Value-based pricing's own definition already requires this: start with value, then check the economics work.
3. **Check the layers do not contradict each other** (see conflicts below).
4. **Run the combined price through the economics**: gross profit per unit, and how much volume the price needs to cover fixed costs (→ [financials](../financials/SKILL.md)).
5. **Test the combination, not just the number.** A bundle discount, a tier structure, or a planned increase changes what customers do.

### Combinations That Work (from the Deck's Examples)

| Combination | How the layers work together | Deck evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Value-Based + Cost-Plus floor + Competitive reference** | Value sets the target, cost proves it is viable, rivals tell you which question (value or quality) the customer will ask. | The three key questions; value-based definition ("check do the economics work"); competitive signal rules. |
| **Penetration + Competitive** | The "low" in penetration is defined relative to rivals. | Streaming launch at $7.99 against a $9.99 market. |
| **Skimming + Value-Based** | Early adopters pay a premium because the value to them is highest; the price steps down to each next layer of buyers. | PS3 $599 → $299 → ~$199; plasma TVs at $10,000+. |
| **Value-Based + Prestige** | Part of what customers pay for is status and brand, so the high price is part of the value. | Apple's value list includes brand and status; Supreme and StanCraft. |
| **Freemium + good-better-best tiers** | The free tier proves value; several paid tiers steer users to a target tier. | Dropbox: free 2GB, paid **tiers** from 2TB. |
| **Bundle + Anchoring** | The separate item prices act as the reference point, so the bundle reads as a deal. | Value Meal priced below the three items bought separately. |
| **Dynamic + Competitive** | Real-time prices respond to supply and demand while staying within sight of rivals; both carry price-war risk, so watch margins. | Airlines, Uber; "risk of price wars" appears in both models' disadvantages. |

### Combinations That Conflict

- **Penetration + Prestige:** penetration risks a "perception of low quality"; prestige depends on a high price signaling quality. On the same product for the same customer, they cancel each other out.
- **Penetration + Skimming on the same product, segment, and time:** they are opposites (low → high vs. high → low). They can coexist only across different products or segments.
- **Skimming in a price-sensitive market:** listed as a skimming disadvantage. If customers have many alternatives (elastic demand), the premium will not hold.
- **Competitive pricing for a product you claim is differentiated:** competitive pricing has "no differentiation"; matching rivals throws away the value you could capture. If you price a lot higher, you must answer the value question.
- **Bundle discounts on items that already sell at full price:** "margin give-away on full-price items."

## Choosing a Pricing Model: The Four Questions

Choose the primary model (and any supporting ones) by working through four areas:

| Area | What to check |
|---|---|
| **Customer** | Is our target correct? Can we describe them? (→ [customer-segmentation](../customer-segmentation/SKILL.md), [customer-end-user-profile-and-persona](../customer-end-user-profile-and-persona/SKILL.md)) |
| **Competitors** | These are the alternatives your customer sees. Know them. (→ [market-research](../market-research/SKILL.md)) |
| **Customer value** | Can you justify the price? How? (→ [value-proposition](../value-proposition/SKILL.md)) |
| **Costs** | Is the price you charge viable? Can you cover costs? (→ [financials](../financials/SKILL.md)) |

**The four questions:**

1. **Who is the customer?**
2. **What alternatives do they have?**
3. **What value do we provide?**
4. **What economics must work?**

### Situation-to-Model Guide

Built from each model's "use when" and "best for." Treat it as a starting shortlist, then run the four questions.

| If the situation is… | Consider as primary | Common supporting layers |
|---|---|---|
| Stable, established market; costs well known | Cost-Plus | Competitive check |
| Differentiated product; branding or innovation matter | Value-Based | Prestige, good-better-best |
| Commodity or highly competitive market; many alternatives (elastic demand) | Competitive | Bundle to grow order size |
| Entering an existing market where volume and traction matter most | Penetration | Competitive reference |
| Innovative, first-to-market product; early adopters exist | Skimming | Value-Based |
| Luxury positioning; brand is the product | Prestige | Value-Based |
| Volatile demand; you can adjust quickly; perishable capacity (seats, rides) | Dynamic | Competitive |
| Digital product where network effects matter | Freemium | Good-better-best paid tiers |
| Complementary products that naturally belong together | Bundle | Anchoring on separate prices |

## Pricing Psychology

**How customers perceive a price affects decisions as much as the price itself.** These techniques shape perception; they do not replace the economics.

### Charm Pricing

- A round price (for example **$59**) reads as a round, thought-out price.
- A price ending in .99 (for example **$59.99**) reads as a bargain: **the brain files it as under $60**.
- **Same product, nearly the same money.** Choose the ending on purpose: .99 for a bargain signal; a round number for a deliberate, premium, or simple signal. Several of the deck's real prices use charm endings: $9.99/month streaming, $999 iPhone, $599 PS3.

### Anchoring

- **The first price seen becomes the reference point.**
- Example: a coffee grinder shown as "was $160, now $120" anchors the customer's brain at $160, so $120 reads as a saving.
- **To work, the reference price must be genuine and acceptable.** A "was" price nobody ever paid is not an anchor; it is a credibility risk.
- Anchoring is also why penetration pricing is risky: the low launch price becomes the anchor every later increase is judged against.

### Good-Better-Best

- **Three tiers.**
- **Steer customers to the target tier.** The top tier makes the middle tier look like the smart choice.
- Example: a scheduling app offers **Starter $19/month, Pro $39/month, Business $79/month**. Pro is the target tier; Business exists partly to make Pro look like the reasonable middle.
- Design rule: decide which tier you want most customers to buy, price and feature it as the middle, and make sure each step up has a reason to pay more.

## Testing Your Price

**Don't guess. Test, measure, adjust. Iterate.**

| Method | What it is |
|---|---|
| **A/B testing** | Compare prices with comparable audiences under similar conditions. |
| **Customer surveys** | Ask willingness to pay at various price points. (Stated willingness is weaker evidence than payment; for testing with the person who pays → [customer-validation](../customer-validation/SKILL.md).) |
| **Pilot markets** | Test pricing in limited areas first. |
| **Monitor metrics** | **Conversion rate, revenue per customer, and profit.** |

Always include **profit** in the metrics. The revenue-vs-profit math above shows a price can lose on revenue and win on profit.

Before testing, ask: **what information are you missing that would help you price?** That gap is what the test should close.

### Price Test Design Template

```
PRICE TEST
Hypothesis:      At $____, [segment] will buy enough that gross profit per
                 period beats the current/alternative price of $____.
Method:          [ ] A/B test  [ ] survey at price points  [ ] pilot market
Audience:        ______________________ (comparable groups, similar conditions)
Price points:    A = $____   B = $____   (C = $____)
Duration/size:   ____ weeks / ____ customers per arm
Metrics:         conversion rate | revenue per customer | gross profit
Decision rule:   Choose B if units(B) / units(A) >= GP per unit(A) / GP per unit(B)
                 (the volume share B needs to match A on gross profit)
Also watch:      repeat purchase, cancellations, complaints about fairness
Next step if it fails: ____________________________________________
```

## Workflow

Run these steps in order. For an audit or re-price, start from the current price as the "opening answer."

1. **Write the opening answer.** Your gut price (or the current price). You will compare against it at the end.
2. **Define what is being priced.** The service, the product, or the experience; list what else the customer is buying (convenience, preparation, location, experience, brand).
3. **Start with the customer.** Name the segment and who pays; confirm you can describe them (→ [customer-segmentation](../customer-segmentation/SKILL.md), [customer-ecosystem](../customer-ecosystem/SKILL.md)).
4. **Compare alternatives.** List every alternative the customer sees, including doing it themselves, with prices (→ [market-research](../market-research/SKILL.md), [problem-statement](../problem-statement/SKILL.md) for workarounds).
5. **Estimate value.** What is this worth to this customer? What benefits justify a price above the alternatives (→ [value-proposition](../value-proposition/SKILL.md))?
6. **Check the economics.** Unit cost (materials + labor + overhead), the cost-plus floor, markup vs. margin at candidate prices, gross profit per unit, and break-even volume (break-even units = fixed costs ÷ (price − variable cost per unit); → [financials](../financials/SKILL.md)).
7. **Read the market.** Run the 8 factors. Apply the four price implications to any expected supply or demand shift. Note dated inflation and price-level context.
8. **Judge elasticity.** Many alternatives → likely elastic; few substitutes → likely inelastic. Predict what a higher or lower price does to quantity, revenue, and gross profit per unit.
9. **Choose the pricing model(s).** Run the four questions and the situation guide; name one primary model and any supporting layers; run the conflict check.
10. **Set the number.** Triangulate floor, reference, and ceiling; apply charm pricing, anchoring, and good-better-best on purpose.
11. **Design the test.** Pick A/B, survey, or pilot; set metrics including profit and a decision rule.
12. **Write the recommendation.** Fill in the template: segment, price, primary approach, one piece of evidence still needed, defense, test, and what changed from the opening answer.
13. **Iterate.** Run the test, measure, adjust, and re-price when costs, alternatives, or demand shift.

## Common Mistakes

- **Pricing on cost alone.** Cost-plus is "built without asking a single customer anything." Use cost as a floor, not the answer.
- **Confusing markup with margin.** A 50% markup is a 33.3% margin. A 50% margin needs a 100% markup.
- **Assuming a higher price means higher profit**, or that more revenue means more profit. Elasticity tells you about revenue; costs determine profit.
- **Treating the market-clearing price as your profit-maximizing price.** They are different views of "right."
- **Changing price without asking how much more or less customers will buy.** That is the elasticity question.
- **Pricing the wrong thing.** Charging for the coffee when you are selling the pickup.
- **Pricing far above rivals without answering the value question**, or far below without answering the quality question.
- **Penetration with no plan for the increase.** Customers anchor low and resist every increase; ask in advance what would make them leave.
- **Penetration without the scale to make thin margins work.** "Requires scale to work" is a listed disadvantage.
- **Skimming in a price-sensitive market**, or forgetting that the high price advertises the market to competitors.
- **Prestige pricing without the experience to support it.** The signal collapses.
- **Dynamic pricing that feels like gouging.** Decide in advance when a changing price feels reasonable and when it feels unfair.
- **Freemium with no clear reason to upgrade.** Free users cost real money to serve; the premium tier must be clearly worth it.
- **Bundles that discount sales you would have made anyway.** Check whether the bundle adds profit or only gives margin away.
- **Fake anchors.** A reference price must be genuine and acceptable.
- **Stacking models that contradict each other** (penetration + prestige on the same product).
- **Reading lower inflation as falling prices.** Inflation slowing means prices rise more slowly; the price level stays higher.
- **Testing on conversion or revenue only.** Always measure profit.
- **Guessing instead of testing.** Test, measure, adjust, iterate.

## Worked Examples

### Positive Example: Cold Brew Concentrate (Illustrative Numbers)

**Situation.** A two-person cold brew company in Houston sells a 32 oz bottle of concentrate (makes 8 drinks) at two weekend farmers' markets and through local delivery. Fixed costs (commercial kitchen rental + market booth fees) are $1,000/month. Expected volume is 500 bottles/month.

**Opening answer:** $12, because "that's what feels fair."

**1. What is being priced:** not coffee, but **8 café-quality drinks with no preparation and no café run**: convenience, preparation, and a local brand.

**2. Customer:** commuters and home-office workers, 25–40, who currently buy 4–5 café cold brews a week; they are the user and the payer.

**3. Alternatives:**

| Alternative | Price |
|---|---|
| Café cold brew, $5.25/cup × 8 | $42.00 per 8 drinks |
| National grocery-store concentrate, 32 oz | $9.49 |
| Brewing at home | ~$0.40/cup, plus a 12–24 hour steep and cleanup |

**4. Value:** against the café, the bottle saves the customer about $26 per 8 drinks at $16. Against the grocery brand, 20 customer interviews pointed to freshness and stronger flavor as the reasons to pay more.

**5. Economics:**

| Item | Per bottle |
|---|---|
| Coffee | $3.10 |
| Bottle, cap, label | $1.40 |
| Production labor | $1.50 |
| **Unit COGS (variable)** | **$6.00** |
| Overhead ($1,000 ÷ 500 bottles) | $2.00 |
| **Full unit cost** | **$8.00** |

- Cost-plus at 50% markup: $8.00 × 1.5 = **$12.00**. (The opening answer was cost-plus instinct in disguise.)
- At **$16**: markup = $8 ÷ $8 = **100%**; margin = $8 ÷ $16 = **50%**; gross profit per unit = $16 − $6 = **$10**.
- Break-even at $16: $1,000 ÷ $10 = **100 bottles/month**. At $12 it would take ~167.

**6. Model choice:** **Value-based primary** (differentiated product, local brand matters), with **cost-plus as the floor** ($12) and **competitive reference** (between the $9.49 grocery bottle and the $42 café equivalent). At $16 the price is a lot higher than the grocery brand, so the defense must answer the value question: freshness, flavor, local. **Supporting layer: bundle.** A 2-pack of Classic + Vanilla Oat (the slower seller, COGS $6.60) at $29 instead of $32. Bundle gross profit = $29 − $12.60 = $16.40. If a bundle buyer would otherwise buy one Classic ($10 GP), the bundle adds $6.40; if they would buy two Classics anyway ($20 GP), it loses $3.60. So the bundle is offered only at the market booth as an add-on to first-time buyers, and its effect is measured.

**7. Psychology:** a **round $16**, not $15.99: a round price reads as thought-out and fits a premium local brand (and makes cash change easy at the booth).

**8. Test:** online delivery orders randomized between $14 and $16 for 4 weeks (comparable audiences, similar conditions). Decision rule: GP per unit is $8 at $14 and $10 at $16, so $16 wins if it sells at least 8 ÷ 10 = **80%** of the units $14 sells.
- Result: $14 → 100 bottles; $16 → 85 bottles.
- Revenue: $1,400 vs. $1,360 (lower price wins on revenue: price +14.3%, quantity −15%, elastic).
- Gross profit: $800 vs. $850 (**higher price wins on profit**). 85% ≥ 80%, so **$16 stands**.

**9. Evidence still needed:** the repeat-purchase rate at $16 over 8 weeks.

**10. What changed from the opening answer:** $12 was cost-plus thinking that ignored the $42 café alternative; the customer comparison and the test moved the price to $16.

**Why this works:**
- It **defines what is being priced** (convenience and preparation, not beans).
- It **answers all three key questions** (cost floor, alternatives, perceived value) and all three "right price" views.
- It **keeps markup and margin straight** and computes gross profit per unit and break-even.
- It **names one primary model** with supporting layers that do not conflict, and it **checks the bundle** against the "would they have bought anyway" risk.
- It uses **charm pricing on purpose**, not by habit.
- Its **test measures profit**, not just revenue, and it reproduces the lesson that elasticity tells you about revenue while costs decide profit.
- It ends with **one piece of evidence still needed** and **what changed**.

### Negative Example: Mobile Dog Grooming (Illustrative Numbers)

**Situation.** A one-van mobile dog-grooming business enters a suburb where established groomers charge about $95 for a full groom. The owner prices it like this:

- "Our cost is supplies $6 + groomer labor (1.5 hours × $22) $33 = **$39**. We want a 50% margin, so we add 50%: **$58.50**." Then, "to undercut everyone," they launch at **$55** and call it penetration pricing.
- The website shows **"Was $120, now $55!"** No one has ever paid $120.
- After two months of full weekends, they add a **1.5× Saturday surge** ($82.50) for everyone, including regulars, with no explanation.
- At month six, they raise the base price to **$85**. Regulars cancel.
- Their "price test" was asking ten friends "would you pay $55?" and tracking bookings.

**Why this is awful:**
- **Incomplete cost-plus.** The formula is (materials + labor + **overhead**) per unit. Van fuel, insurance, and van payment add about $14 per appointment, so the true unit cost is **$53**, and the $55 price leaves about **$2** per groom.
- **Markup confused with margin.** A 50% markup on $39 is a 33.3% margin, not 50%. A 50% margin needed a 100% markup ($78 on $39, or $106 on the true $53).
- **Penetration without scale.** One van can do about 5 grooms a day, so ~$10/day after overhead. Penetration "requires scale to work"; this business cannot reach it.
- **A lot lower than rivals raises a quality question.** At 42% below the $95 market, owners of expensive breeds assumed the work was rushed.
- **Fake anchor.** "To work the reference price must be genuine and acceptable." A $120 "was" price that never existed undermines trust.
- **Dynamic pricing that feels like gouging.** A surge applied to loyal regulars without a visible supply reason produced "customer dissatisfaction" and "perceived unfairness," both listed disadvantages.
- **Conflicting layers and no increase plan.** Customers anchored on $55 and resisted the jump to $85, the exact biggest risk of penetration, and nobody asked beforehand what would make them leave.
- **Revenue mistaken for success.** Full weekends meant rising revenue while profit stayed near zero: costs determine profit.
- **No real test.** Friends are not the paying customers, a survey of stated willingness is weak evidence, and bookings are not profit.

**How to fix it:** compute the full unit cost ($53); price against the $95 reference with a value-based primary model (convenience of at-home grooming is the differentiator), for example good-better-best tiers of Bath $65 / Full Groom $95 / Full Groom + De-shed $125; drop the fake anchor; apply surge pricing only to new same-day bookings, if at all; and A/B test two Full Groom prices on new customers, measuring gross profit per van-day.
