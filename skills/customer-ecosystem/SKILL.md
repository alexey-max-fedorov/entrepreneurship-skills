# Customer Ecosystem

You are an expert in customer ecosystems and stakeholder roles in purchase and adoption decisions. Your goal is to help the user identify every person who touches the decision to adopt and buy their product, understand what role each one plays, and match the right message and evidence to each role.

## The Core Idea: A Customer Is Not Always One Person

The **customer ecosystem** is the network of people who surround and influence a customer's purchase decision. Together, these people form a connected network that shapes **adoption** and **purchase decisions**.

Three truths drive the whole concept:

- **Your customer rarely decides alone.**
- **Different people play different roles in the buying process.**
- **Understanding the ecosystem helps you target the right message to the right person.**

> You are not always selling to one person; you may be navigating a web of relationships, influence, and decision authority.

**Multiple stakeholders surround every purchase decision.** Treat this as the governing rule of the ecosystem model. Even when one person appears to do everything, assume there are other voices shaping the outcome until you have checked.

## Stakeholder Roles (The Six Customer Types)

Every ecosystem is built from six roles. Identify all of them for any product.

| Role | Short definition | Full definition | The question to answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| **End User** | Day-to-day product user | The person who will actually use your product or service | Who touches it every day? |
| **Decision Maker** | Makes the purchase decision | The person with authority to approve or reject the purchase | Who says yes or no? |
| **Economic Buyer** | Controls budget / signs checks | The person who controls the budget and signs the check | Whose money is it? |
| **Influencer** | Can sway the decision | People whose opinions carry weight in the decision | Whose opinion does the decision maker listen to? |
| **Champion** | Pushes for the purchase | Internal advocate who actively promotes your solution | Who will fight for this when you are not in the room? |
| **Blocker** | Prevents or delays adoption | A person with the power to delay or stop adoption | Who can say no, or slow this down, even without a budget? |

### End User

- The center of the ecosystem: the person who lives with the product day to day.
- Their experience drives adoption, retention, and word of mouth, but they often lack purchase authority in B2B.
- Build their detailed profile and persona separately (→ [customer-end-user-profile-and-persona](../customer-end-user-profile-and-persona/SKILL.md)).

### Decision Maker

- Holds the authority to approve or reject the purchase.
- Is not necessarily the user, and not necessarily the person who controls the money.

### Economic Buyer

- Controls the budget and signs the check.
- Can be a different person from the decision maker. In the CRM example below, the VP of Sales decides and the CFO pays.

### Influencer

- Anyone whose opinion carries weight. Influencers can be people inside the organization (IT team, ops manager) or outside it (friends, reviewers, social media personalities, salespeople).
- In B2C, influencers often do most of the persuading for you or against you.

### Champion

- An **internal advocate** who actively promotes your solution.
- The champion is distinct from a mere fan: they push for the purchase inside the buying group, carry your case into meetings you cannot attend, and spend their own credibility on it.

### Blocker

- Has the power to delay or stop adoption.
- Blockers often have no say in whether the product is wanted, only in whether it is allowed (security, compliance, legal, procurement, a skeptical spouse). Ignoring them is how deals that "everyone loved" die.

## The Customer Ecosystem Model

The model places the roles in rings around the purchase decision:

```
        Influencer                                   Blocker
                    +-----------------------------+
                    |       Decision Maker        |
                    |    +-------------------+    |
        Champion    |    |     END USER      |    |  Influencer
                    |    +-------------------+    |
                    |       Economic Buyer        |
                    +-----------------------------+

          Multiple stakeholders surround every purchase decision
```

How to read it:

- **Center: the End User.** The product exists for this person.
- **Inner ring: Decision Maker and Economic Buyer.** The formal authority and the money sit closest to the user.
- **Outside the rings: Influencers, Champion, Blocker.** They are not formally in charge, but they push the decision toward yes (champion, positive influencers) or toward no or delay (blocker, negative influencers). Influencers appear on both sides because their opinions can cut either way.

## Why You Need to Identify All Customer Types

Understanding the ecosystem matters for four concrete reasons:

1. **Target your message.** Each role cares about different things; one generic pitch fits none of them well.
2. **Identify approvers and blockers.** Know who can say yes and who can stop or delay you before you are surprised by them.
3. **Know who to interview.** Discovery that only reaches end users misses the people who pay, approve, and veto (→ [customer-interview](../customer-interview/SKILL.md)).
4. **Match evidence and message to each role.** Different roles need different proof: a user wants to see that it works in their day; a payer wants to see the return; a blocker wants to see the risk handled.

## B2C vs. B2B Ecosystems

The same six roles exist in both, but the shape of the ecosystem differs. These are typical characteristics, not rules; check them against the real customer.

| Dimension | B2C (typically) | B2B (typically) |
|---|---|---|
| **Complexity** | **Simpler:** fewer stakeholders | **Complex:** often involves multiple stakeholders |
| **Speed** | **Faster:** quicker decision cycles | **Slower:** months-long cycles (decision cycles can range from weeks to months) |
| **Decision logic** | **Emotional:** feelings may drive choices | **Rational:** often emphasizes ROI, risk, workflow fit, and evidence |
| **Who decides** | **Individual:** the user and buyer are often the same person | **Committee:** consensus required |

### B2C Customer Ecosystem

In B2C, several roles often collapse into one person: the end user, decision maker, and economic buyer can all be the same individual. The ecosystem does not disappear; it moves outward into influencers.

**Slide example: buying running shoes**

- **End user and buyer:** often the same person (the runner).
- **Influencers:**
  - Friend recommendations
  - Online reviews and ratings
  - Social media influencers
  - Store salesperson

Watch for B2C cases where roles split: a parent pays for a child's product, a partner vetoes a household purchase, a gift buyer chooses for the user. Map these the same way.

### What Influences B2C Purchase Decisions

B2C influence comes in three groups. Audit all three for any consumer product.

| Social influences | Psychological factors | Situational context |
|---|---|---|
| Friends and family opinions | Personal preferences | Urgency of need |
| Social media validation | Brand loyalty | Promotions and discounts |
| Influencer endorsements | Emotional appeal | Convenience factors |
| Community trends | Past experiences | Availability |

- **Social influences** are other people: map them as the influencer role.
- **Psychological factors** live inside the buyer: they shape which message lands.
- **Situational context** is the moment of purchase: it shapes timing, channel, and offer.

### B2B Customer Ecosystem

In B2B, roles usually belong to different people, and the decision needs consensus. Each role weighs **ROI, risk, workflow fit, and evidence** differently, so you need a separate case for each.

**Slide example: CRM software purchase**

| Role | Person |
|---|---|
| End Users | Sales team members |
| Decision Maker | VP of Sales |
| Economic Buyer | CFO |
| Influencers | IT team, ops manager |
| Blocker | Security officer |

Decision cycle: can range from weeks to months.

Notice what this example teaches:

- The people who use the CRM every day (sales reps) neither approve it nor pay for it.
- The decision maker and the economic buyer are different people with different concerns (sales performance vs. budget and return).
- The security officer has nothing to do with selling, yet can stop the deal outright.
- No champion is listed. When you map a real deal, a missing champion is a gap to fill, not a detail to skip.

## Mapping the Ecosystem: Templates

### Template 1: Ecosystem Map

Fill one row per role. A role may be held by several people, one person may hold several roles, and a row may be "none found yet" (which is itself a finding).

```
PRODUCT:            [what it is]
SEGMENT:            [specific target customer segment]
CONTEXT:            [B2C / B2B / both]
EXPECTED CYCLE:     [days / weeks / months]

ROLE             | WHO (title or relationship) | WHAT THEY CARE ABOUT | THEIR POWER (approve / pay / sway / push / stop) | CONFIRMED? (talked to them / assumed)
-----------------|-----------------------------|----------------------|--------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------
End User         |                             |                      |                                                  |
Decision Maker   |                             |                      |                                                  |
Economic Buyer   |                             |                      |                                                  |
Influencer(s)    |                             |                      |                                                  |
Champion         |                             |                      |                                                  |
Blocker(s)       |                             |                      |                                                  |
```

### Template 2: Message and Evidence by Role

This turns the map into action: the right message and the right proof for each person.

```
ROLE            | THEIR KEY QUESTION                         | MESSAGE (what we say)  | EVIDENCE (what we show)
----------------|--------------------------------------------|------------------------|------------------------
End User        | Will this make my day easier?              |                        |
Decision Maker  | Should we do this?                         |                        |
Economic Buyer  | Is it worth the money?                     |                        |
Influencer      | Would I recommend this?                    |                        |
Champion        | Can I win the internal argument with this? |                        |
Blocker         | Is it safe / compliant / allowed?          |                        |
```

For B2B, make sure the evidence column covers **ROI, risk, workflow fit, and evidence**. For B2C, make sure the message column addresses the **social, psychological, and situational** influences that apply.

### Template 3: B2C Influence Audit

```
SOCIAL         Friends & family:        [who, what they'd say]
               Social media validation: [where buyers look]
               Influencer endorsements: [which voices matter]
               Community trends:        [what's trending in this group]

               Brand loyalty:           [incumbent brands they're loyal to]
               Emotional appeal:        [the feeling that drives the purchase]
               Past experiences:        [what burned or delighted them before]

SITUATIONAL    Urgency of need:         [now vs. someday]
               Promotions & discounts:  [...]
               Convenience factors:     [...]
               Availability:            [where and when they can get it]
```

## Workflow

Use this sequence whether you are starting from scratch, auditing an existing go-to-market, or diagnosing why deals stall.

1. **Anchor the segment and problem.** Confirm who the target customer segment is and what problem you solve for them (→ [customer-segmentation](../customer-segmentation/SKILL.md), [problem-statement](../problem-statement/SKILL.md)).
2. **Classify the context.** B2C, B2B, or mixed. Set expectations for number of stakeholders, cycle length, and emotional vs. rational logic using the B2C/B2B table.
3. **Place the end user at the center.** Name the person who will actually use the product day to day.
4. **Fill the inner ring.** Who has authority to approve or reject? Who controls the budget and signs the check? Check whether these are the same person or different people.
5. **Fill the outer ring.** List influencers (inside and outside the organization), the champion (or the person most likely to become one), and every possible blocker.
6. **For B2C, run the influence audit.** Social, psychological, and situational factors (Template 3).
7. **Mark each entry as confirmed or assumed.** Anything assumed becomes an interview target (→ [customer-interview](../customer-interview/SKILL.md)).
8. **Build the message and evidence matrix.** One message and one set of proof per role (Template 2). Tailor the value proposition per role (→ [value-proposition](../value-proposition/SKILL.md)).
9. **Plan around approvers and blockers.** Decide how you will earn each approval and neutralize or satisfy each blocker before they surface on their own.
10. **Validate with every role, not just users.** Confirm that the payer will pay and the approvers will approve (→ [customer-validation](../customer-validation/SKILL.md)).
11. **Revisit when anything changes.** A new segment, a new price point, a move from B2C to B2B, or a lost deal all mean the map needs updating.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating the customer as one person.** The core error this skill exists to catch. Multiple stakeholders surround every purchase decision.
- **Confusing the end user with the buyer.** Users love it, nobody with budget has heard of it.
- **Confusing the decision maker with the economic buyer.** Winning the VP of Sales does not mean the CFO will release the money.
- **Ignoring blockers.** Security, compliance, legal, procurement, or a skeptical family member can stop or delay a purchase everyone else wanted.
- **Mistaking a fan for a champion.** Someone who likes the product is not a champion until they actively push for it internally.
- **One pitch for everyone.** Sending the same message and evidence to every role, instead of matching evidence and message to each role.
- **Interviewing only end users.** Discovery that never reaches approvers, payers, and blockers produces false confidence.
- **Assuming B2C has no ecosystem.** User and buyer may be one person, but friends, reviews, influencers, and salespeople still sway the decision.
- **Pitching B2B on emotion alone.** B2B buyers often emphasize ROI, risk, workflow fit, and evidence, and need consensus.
- **Planning a B2B launch on a B2C timeline.** B2B cycles are slower, ranging from weeks to months; cash runway and forecasts need to reflect that.
- **Leaving roles as "assumed" forever.** A map built from guesses is a hypothesis, not a finding.

## Worked Examples

### Positive Example: Shift-Scheduling Software for Hospital Nursing Units

**Context.** A startup sells shift-scheduling software to mid-sized community hospitals. Nurses currently swap shifts through group texts and a paper board, and units are chronically short-staffed on weekends. This is B2B, so the founders expect a committee and a cycle of months, not days.

**Ecosystem map (built after 25 conversations across all roles):**

| Role | Who | What they care about | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| End User | Staff nurses and charge nurses | Fair schedules, easy shift swaps from a phone, fewer last-minute calls | Confirmed (12 nurses interviewed) |
| Decision Maker | Director of Nursing | Coverage, burnout, retention of nurses | Confirmed |
| Economic Buyer | Hospital CFO | Overtime and agency-staffing spend, payback period | Confirmed |
| Influencers | Nurse managers, HR/staffing office, nurses' union representative | Workload for managers; payroll accuracy; fairness of shift assignment rules | Confirmed |
| Champion | A night-shift nurse manager who spends hours each week rebuilding schedules | Getting her time back; being the person who fixed it | Confirmed, actively pushing |
| Blocker | Hospital IT security / compliance officer | Data security, integration with existing systems | Confirmed, concerns documented |

**Message and evidence by role:**

- **Nurses:** "Swap a shift from your phone in under a minute." Evidence: a live demo on a phone and a two-week pilot on one unit.
- **Director of Nursing:** "Fewer uncovered shifts and fewer burned-out nurses." Evidence: coverage results from the pilot unit compared with a similar unit.
- **CFO:** "Lower overtime and agency spend." Evidence: an ROI model built from the hospital's own overtime figures, with the assumptions shown.
- **Union rep and HR:** "Your assignment rules are built in and auditable." Evidence: a walkthrough of how seniority and fairness rules are configured.
- **Champion:** A one-page internal case she can forward, plus pilot data she can present herself.
- **Security officer:** Security documentation and an integration plan delivered before he asked for it.

**Why this works:**

- Every one of the six roles is identified by a real title and person, and every row is **confirmed**, not assumed.
- The **decision maker and economic buyer are separated**, and each gets a case in their own terms (coverage vs. dollars).
- The pitch to the CFO is **rational: ROI, risk, workflow fit, evidence**, as B2B logic requires.
- The **blocker is engaged early**, so the deal is not ambushed late in the cycle.
- The **champion is equipped** to sell internally when the founders are not in the room.
- Interviews covered **every role**, not only nurses, so the founders know who to interview next and what each needs.
- The founders planned their runway for a **months-long cycle**.

### Negative Example: K-12 Math Practice App Sold to School Districts

**Context.** A founder builds a gamified math practice app for middle schoolers and plans to sell it to public school districts.

**What the founder did:**

- Called **students "the customer."** Ran discovery only with students at a coding camp, who loved the game mechanics.
- Pitched every audience the same way: "Kids love it, it's fun, it's addictive."
- Sent cold emails to a district superintendent with a link to the app store page.
- Expected to close the first districts within a few weeks.
- Never asked who handles student data privacy.

**What the ecosystem actually looked like:**

| Role | Who it really was | What the founder missed |
|---|---|---|
| End Users | Students **and** math teachers (who assign work and read the reports) | Teachers were never interviewed; the app had no teacher dashboard |
| Decision Maker | District curriculum director | Cares about alignment with standards and learning outcomes, not "fun" |
| Economic Buyer | District business office / budget committee | Needed per-student pricing and a budget line, which the founder could not provide |
| Influencers | Teachers, principals, parents | Parents worried about screen time and "addictive" language |
| Champion | None identified | No one inside the district was pushing for it |
| Blocker | District IT / student data privacy officer | Stopped the pilot because no data privacy agreement existed |

**Why this fails:**

- **One person treated as the whole customer.** Students are end users, but they neither approve, pay, nor block.
- **No message matched to roles.** "Addictive" is a selling point to a 12-year-old and a red flag to a parent and a curriculum director.
- **Wrong decision logic.** A B2B (institutional) buyer needs **evidence, risk handling, and workflow fit**, and the founder pitched emotion.
- **Blocker discovered last.** The data privacy officer could stop adoption outright and was found only after the pilot was approved.
- **No champion.** Without an internal advocate, the proposal had no one to carry it through committee.
- **B2C timeline on a B2B ecosystem.** Expecting weeks when consensus-driven cycles run for months left the company short on runway.
- **Interviews never reached the people who decide.** The founder did not know who to interview, so confident early signals were false.
