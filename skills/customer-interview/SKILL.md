## Before You Start

Establish these before writing questions or starting a conversation. Ask the user for anything missing.

1. **Which mode?** Training/briefing an entrepreneur, writing a script or starting questions, conducting the interview yourself, reviewing an existing script, or synthesizing completed interviews. More than one is fine.
2. **What [problem/issue] are you exploring?** Interviews explore a problem, not a product. If the user only has a solution, help them name the underlying problem first (→ [problem-statement](../problem-statement/SKILL.md)).
3. **Who are you interviewing?** The target customer type and profile, so you can later record whether each interviewee matches (→ [customer-segmentation](../customer-segmentation/SKILL.md), [customer-end-user-profile-and-persona](../customer-end-user-profile-and-persona/SKILL.md)). Note whether the buyer and the user are different people (→ [customer-ecosystem](../customer-ecosystem/SKILL.md)).
4. **What are your assumptions?** Write them down now. After every interview you record the implications for each one; you cannot do that if they were never stated.
5. **Logistics.** How much time you are asking for (the slide guide asks for about 10 minutes), the channel (in person, call, chat), and how the conversation will be captured so exact quotes survive.

## Why Customer Interviews Matter

A customer interview is a conversation that gets a real person to tell you about their actual experience with a problem: what happened, why, what they did about it, and what got in the way. Done well, it gives you **evidence** (exact quotes, specific situations, current alternatives, signs of urgency) that lets you make a real decision about the idea: **Proceed, Pivot, or Punt**.

Done badly (pitching, leading, asking for generalities), it produces polite agreement that feels like validation and is worth nothing.

## The Customer Interview Rules

Ten rules, grouped the way the slides summarize them.

| Group | Rules |
|---|---|
| **Ask the right questions** | Open-ended questions; follow-up questions; avoid leading questions; ask about specific situations |
| **Listen & stay neutral** | Listen actively; stay nonjudgmental; be respectful |
| **Best practices** | Ask about alternatives; avoid jargon; keep the conversation flowing (keep it easy) |

### Ask the Right Questions

#### Rule 1: Ask Open-Ended Questions

Open-ended questions cannot be answered with yes or no; they make the interviewee describe. Start them with:

- **"What..."**
- **"How..."**
- **"Why..."**
- **"Tell me about..."**

Slide stems:

- "Can you tell me about your experience with [problem/issue]?"
- "How do you currently handle [problem/issue]?"

#### Rule 2: Ask Follow-Up Questions

The first answer is the surface. Follow up to explore three things:

- **Motivations** — why they do what they do
- **Challenges** — what gets in the way
- **Preferences** — what they choose and why

Slide stems:

- "Can you explain why you feel that way?"
- "What impact does [problem/issue] have on your daily life?"

#### Rule 3: Avoid Leading Questions

Do not "lead" or "suggest" an answer. A leading question tells the interviewee what you want to hear, and most people will politely give it to you.

| Don't say | Do say |
|---|---|
| "Don't you think our product (describe product) is the best solution?" | "What are your thoughts on potential solutions in this area?" |

Leading cues to strip out: "Don't you think...", "Wouldn't you agree...", describing your product before asking, and any question whose wording contains the answer.

#### Rule 4: Ask About Specific Situations

- Encourage the interviewee to **recall specific experiences/situations**.
- Specific situations provide the **"best" insights**.
- **Not generalities.** "I usually..." and "people like me..." are opinions about a pattern; a specific recent event is evidence.

Slide stem:

- "Can you share a recent example of when you encountered [problem/issue]?"

### Listen & Stay Neutral

#### Rule 5: Listen Actively

- **Listen to the response.**
- **Prepare your next question based on what you heard**, not on what is next on your list.

Slide illustration:

| They said | Strong next question |
|---|---|
| "I typically like t-shirts that absorb sweat." | "Why ones that absorb sweat?" (motivation) |
| | "When was the last time you wore a t-shirt that absorbed sweat?" (specific situation) |

Notice that both follow-ups reuse the interviewee's own words and push either toward **why** or toward a **specific instance**.

#### Rule 6: Stay Neutral (Nonjudgmental)

- Create a **"safe" environment**.
- **Let interviewees express their opinion.**
- **Avoid inserting your own judgment.**

| Don't say |
|---|
| "While I hear you like t-shirts that absorb sweat, don't you think a more important criteria is how it makes you look?" |

That question both judges their preference and leads them to a different one. The interviewee's criteria are the data; your criteria are irrelevant.

#### Rule 7: Be Respectful

- **Don't make them uncomfortable.**
- **Respect personal boundaries.**
- **Avoid "sensitive" information.**

| Don't ask | Ask |
|---|---|
| "What makes you such a failure in relationships?" | "What would help you be more successful in relationships?" |

The reframe keeps the same topic but points it at what would help, not at a personal flaw.

### Best Practices

#### Rule 8: Ask About Alternatives

- **Inquire about alternate solutions.**
- These could be **what they used in the past**, or **something they were thinking of** trying.
- This **helps you understand the competitive landscape** (→ [market-research](../market-research/SKILL.md)).

Slide stem:

- "Have you explored other ways to address [problem/issue]?"

The current alternative is also what your idea must beat, so always capture it in the evidence record.

#### Rule 9: Avoid Jargon

- **Clear language!**
- **Avoid "buzz" words or industry terms.**
- **Do not confuse or intimidate** (or make them "feel" dumb).

The slides' examples of the kind of language that loses people: "blockchain algorithm," "Einstein-Rosen bridge," "quantum entanglement." Your own industry terms do the same damage even when they sound ordinary to you. Use the words the interviewee uses.

#### Rule 10: Keep the Conversation Easy (Flowing)

- **Don't follow a questionnaire.** Your guide is a map, not a script to read top to bottom.
- **Keep the interview casual.**
- **Allow for unexpected twists and turns.** The surprise is often the insight.

**Goal:** it should feel like you are having a drink on the back porch, having a discussion with a friend.

## The Interview Guide

An interview guide is the short list of questions you bring into the conversation, each tagged with **why you are asking it**. The tag is for you, never read aloud; it tells you what you must learn, so you can wander (Rule 10) and still come back with the evidence you need.

### Anatomy of a Guide

Derived from the slides' sample guide ("Stitch Fix for Dogs," a dog-owner clothing and gear idea):

| Element | Slide's version | What it does (the hidden purpose) |
|---|---|---|
| **Honest intro + permission** | "Hi, I'm Steve, a UH student learning about how dog owners shop for products for their dogs. I'm not selling anything. Would you be willing to share your experiences for about 10 minutes?" | Says who you are, frames it as learning, explicitly says you are **not selling**, asks permission, and states the time. |
| **Context about them** | "Can you tell me a little about your dog?" | *Find out about weight/size to test whether a market exists.* A friendly opener that also screens for your target profile. |
| **Last specific occurrence** | "Tell me about the last time you bought clothing, accessories, or protective gear for your dog." | *Purchasing patterns.* Anchors on a specific situation (Rule 4). |
| **Trigger** | "What prompted that purchase?" | *Why.* Motivation (Rule 2). |
| **Decision process** | "How did you decide what to buy and where to buy it?" | *Online shopping versus in person.* Process and channel. |
| **Friction** | "What was frustrating or difficult, if anything?" | *What is the process, pain points.* Note "if anything": it leaves room for "nothing," which keeps it non-leading. |
| **Alternatives** | "What have you tried instead?" | *Other alternatives* (Rule 8). |

### Fill-In Guide Template

```
INTERVIEW GUIDE: [working name of the idea — never said aloud]
Problem/issue explored: [problem in the customer's words]
Target interviewee: [customer type and profile]
Assumptions being tested:
  A1. [assumption]
  A2. [assumption]
  A3. [assumption]

INTRO (say this)
"Hi, I'm [name], [who you are / affiliation], learning about how [type of person]
[do the activity where the problem lives]. I'm not selling anything. Would you be
willing to share your experiences for about [time] minutes?"

QUESTIONS (purpose in parentheses — do not read aloud)
1. Can you tell me a little about [their situation / role / household / business]?
   (screen: profile match; [what detail tells you the market exists])
2. Tell me about the last time you [did the activity / ran into the problem].
   (specific situation; tests A_)
3. What prompted that?
   (why / motivation)
4. How did you decide what to do [and where / who / which tool]?
   (decision process; channel; preferences)
5. What was frustrating or difficult, if anything?
   (process, pain points; tests A_)
6. What have you tried instead? / Have you explored other ways to address it?
   (alternatives, past or considered; competitive landscape)
7. What impact does [problem/issue] have on your [day / work / budget]?
   (urgency; tests A_)

FOLLOW-UP PROBES (use on anything interesting)
- Can you explain why you feel that way?
- When was the last time that happened?
- What happened next?

CLOSE
Thank them. [Optional: ask if you may follow up later.]
```

### Script Quality Checklist

Run every question in a draft (yours or the user's) through this before it goes into an interview:

- [ ] **Open-ended?** Starts with What / How / Why / Tell me about; cannot be answered yes/no.
- [ ] **Non-leading?** No "don't you think," no product description, no answer embedded in the wording.
- [ ] **Specific?** Asks about a real, recent situation, not a generality.
- [ ] **Jargon-free?** Would the interviewee use these words themselves?
- [ ] **Respectful?** No sensitive information, no implied judgment of the person.
- [ ] **Purpose-tagged?** You know which assumption or evidence field this question feeds.
- [ ] **Short enough?** The whole guide fits the time you asked for, with room for follow-ups.
- [ ] **Problem before solution?** Any solution question comes late and is neutral ("What are your thoughts on potential solutions in this area?").

When reviewing a user's script, rewrite each failing question and say which rule it broke.

## Briefing or Training an Entrepreneur on Customer Interviews

When the user wants to learn to interview (or wants you to brief a teammate):

1. **Start with the purpose.** The interview exists to collect evidence about a problem so they can decide Proceed, Pivot, or Punt. It is not a sales call and not a chance to defend the idea.
2. **Teach the ten rules in their three groups** (Ask the right questions → Listen & stay neutral → Best practices), each with its Don't/Do pair from above.
3. **Drill the two hardest habits:**
   - **Converting leading questions into neutral ones.** Give them leading questions from their own domain and have them rewrite each.
   - **Following up on what they heard.** Give them an interviewee line (like "I typically like t-shirts that absorb sweat") and have them produce a "why" follow-up and a "last time" follow-up.
4. **Have them build their own guide** with the template, purpose tags included, then run it through the checklist.
5. **Role-play.** Play the interviewee in their target segment. Answer realistically: sometimes vague, sometimes off-topic, sometimes with no pain at all. Afterward, point out each rule they followed or broke, quoting their exact words.
6. **Teach the after-interview record** and the Proceed / Pivot / Punt decision so they know what the conversation has to produce.

## Writing Interview Scripts and Starting Questions

When the user wants a script or a set of starting questions:

1. Confirm the problem/issue, the target interviewee, and the assumptions (see Before You Start).
2. Fill in the guide template. Use the interviewee's language, not the founder's.
3. Tag every question with its purpose and the assumption it tests.
4. Add 2-3 follow-up probes the interviewer can reuse on anything interesting.
5. Run the checklist; fix failures.
6. Remind the user that the guide is a map: they should not read it as a questionnaire, and should chase unexpected twists.
7. Hand over the blank evidence record (below) so they fill it in after every interview.

| Specific situation | "Can you share a recent example of when you encountered [problem/issue]?" / "Tell me about the last time you [activity]." |
| Motivation | "What prompted that?" / "Can you explain why you feel that way?" |
| Process and preferences | "How did you decide what to [buy / use / do] and where?" |
| Challenges / pain | "What was frustrating or difficult, if anything?" / "What impact does [problem/issue] have on your daily life?" |
| Alternatives | "What have you tried instead?" / "Have you explored other ways to address [problem/issue]?" |
| Solution space (late, neutral) | "What are your thoughts on potential solutions in this area?" |
| Sensitive topics, reframed | "What would help you be more successful in [area]?" |

## Conducting a Customer Interview Yourself (Agent as Interviewer)

When you are the interviewer in a live conversation, the same ten rules apply, translated into conversational behavior:

- **Open with the honest intro.** Say who you are and who the research is for, that you are learning and not selling anything, roughly how long it takes, and ask whether they are willing. If you are an AI, say so in the intro. If they decline, thank them and stop.
- **One question per message.** Keep each turn short and casual (Rule 10). No numbered lists of questions; that is a questionnaire.
- **Anchor on a specific situation early** ("Tell me about the last time...") and return to it whenever answers drift into generalities.
- **Follow up on their exact words** before moving on (Rules 2 and 5). Your next question should usually be built from their last answer.
- **Never pitch, never evaluate.** Do not describe the product, do not praise or correct their choices, do not say "great answer" in a way that signals what you want more of. Simple acknowledgments are fine.
- **If they ask what you think or what you are building,** keep the focus on them: you can say you are still learning and ask "What are your thoughts on potential solutions in this area?"
- **Use their vocabulary**, not industry terms (Rule 9).
- **Respect boundaries.** If they hesitate or a topic turns personal, reframe toward what would help, or move on (Rule 7).
- **Cover the guide's purposes, not its wording.** Track internally which evidence fields you have (situation, problem, current alternative, urgency, profile match) and steer gently toward the missing ones.
- **Allow twists.** If they raise a different problem with more energy than yours, follow it; it may signal a Pivot.
- **Close** by thanking them and respecting the time you promised.
- **Afterward, produce the evidence record** below for the user, with exact quotes.

## Interviews to Evidence

After **every** interview, record:

| Field | What to capture |
|---|---|
| **Customer type and profile match** | Who they are, and whether they match your target profile (→ [customer-end-user-profile-and-persona](../customer-end-user-profile-and-persona/SKILL.md)). |
| **Exact quote** | Their words, verbatim, not your paraphrase. |
| **Situation and problem** | The specific situation they described and the problem it revealed. |
| **Current alternative** | What they use or do today, or tried before, or were thinking of trying. |
| **Evidence of urgency** | What in their story shows how pressing the problem is (for example, what they have already done about it, or what it costs them). |
| **Implications for your assumptions** | For each stated assumption: supported, contradicted, or untouched, and why. |

Then, across interviews: **look for repeated patterns, not one memorable comment.** One vivid story is an anecdote; the same situation, pain, or workaround showing up across several interviewees is evidence.

### Evidence Record Template

```
INTERVIEW #[n] — [date] — [channel]
Interviewee: [type / role]           Profile match: [Yes / Partial / No — why]
Exact quote(s):
  "[verbatim]"
  "[verbatim]"
Situation and problem: [specific event they described → problem it shows]
Current alternative: [what they do / used / considered]
Evidence of urgency: [what shows it matters, or "none observed"]
Implications for assumptions:
  A1 [assumption]: [supported / contradicted / no evidence] — [why]
  A2 [assumption]: [...]
Surprises / twists: [anything unexpected worth following]
```

```
PATTERN SUMMARY — after [n] interviews
Repeated situations/problems: [pattern] — seen in [x] of [n]
Repeated alternatives: [pattern] — seen in [x] of [n]
Urgency signals: [pattern] — seen in [x] of [n]
Assumptions: A1 [status], A2 [status], A3 [status]
One-off comments (do not weigh heavily): [list]
Decision: [Proceed / Pivot / Punt] — because [pattern evidence]
```

## Evidence to Decision: Proceed, Pivot, or Punt

The Proceed / Pivot / Punt decision is defined in → [problem-statement](../problem-statement/SKILL.md). Here it is applied to interview evidence, with the criteria from this stage. The interviews end in one of three calls:

| Decision | When the evidence shows | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| **Proceed** | Customers **validate the need and your idea**. | Keep going with the idea (→ [customer-validation](../customer-validation/SKILL.md)). |
| **Pivot** | Customer evidence **reveals a different customer, problem, or need**. | **Adapt the idea** to what the evidence shows (re-target via [customer-segmentation](../customer-segmentation/SKILL.md), or re-frame via [problem-statement](../problem-statement/SKILL.md)). |
| **Punt** | There is **not a meaningful need or sufficient interest**. | **Stop, or choose a different problem** (→ [opportunity-id](../opportunity-id/SKILL.md)). |

Base the call on the pattern summary, not on the most enthusiastic interviewee. A Punt reached early is a good outcome: it is cheap compared with building something nobody needs.

## Workflow

1. **Frame.** Name the problem/issue, the target interviewee, and your written assumptions.
2. **Recruit.** Choose interviewees who match the target profile (and the relevant stakeholder roles). How many interviews to run (5–10 per segment, until answers repeat), where to find interviewees, and why friends and family don't count are owned by → [customer-validation](../customer-validation/SKILL.md).
3. **Build the guide.** Honest intro, purpose-tagged open questions, follow-up probes; pass the checklist.
4. **Interview.** Follow the ten rules; treat the guide as a map; chase twists.
5. **Record.** Fill in the evidence record immediately after every interview, with exact quotes.
6. **Synthesize.** Look for repeated patterns across interviews, not memorable one-offs; update each assumption's status.
7. **Decide.** Proceed, Pivot, or Punt.
8. **Route.** Take the decision to the next skill (see Related Skills & Next Steps). On a Pivot, revise the guide and interview again.

## Common Mistakes

- **Pitching instead of learning.** Describing the product turns the interview into a sales call and every answer into politeness. Say "I'm not selling anything," and mean it.
- **Leading questions.** "Don't you think..." / "Wouldn't it be great if..." manufacture agreement.
- **Yes/no questions.** "Do you have trouble with X?" gets "yeah, sometimes" and nothing else.
- **Generalities instead of specific situations.** "How do you usually..." produces an idealized story; "Tell me about the last time..." produces evidence.
- **Reading the questionnaire.** Moving to question 4 while they are mid-story about something more important.
- **Not following up.** Accepting the first answer instead of asking why or when.
- **Judging their preferences** or correcting them toward what you think should matter.
- **Jargon.** Your industry words confuse or intimidate, and confused people agree to end the confusion.
- **Probing sensitive topics** or framing questions around their failures.
- **Skipping alternatives.** Without the current alternative you do not know what you have to beat.
- **Paraphrasing instead of quoting.** Your paraphrase drifts toward what you hoped to hear.
- **Deciding on one memorable comment.** One enthusiastic person is not a pattern.
- **Not stating assumptions first,** so nothing can be confirmed or contradicted.

## Worked Examples

Both examples use the same founder: Priya, who suspects that owners of small independent restaurants waste money on over-ordered produce because they order by gut feel. Her assumptions: **A1** owners order produce by gut feel; **A2** spoiled produce is a significant, noticed cost; **A3** current tools (spreadsheets, supplier apps) don't solve it.

### Positive Example: A Strong Interview

**Interviewee:** Marisol, owner-operator of a 40-seat taqueria.

> **Priya:** Hi, I'm Priya. I'm researching how small restaurant owners handle ordering produce. I'm not selling anything. Would you be willing to share your experience for about 10 minutes?
> *Why it works: honest intro, learning frame, "not selling," permission, time stated.*
>
> **Marisol:** Sure, before the lunch rush.
>
> **Priya:** Can you tell me a little about the restaurant and who does the ordering?
> *Why: context opener that checks profile match (owner who orders = target).*
>
> **Marisol:** It's me. I order twice a week from one supplier, by text mostly.
>
> **Priya:** Tell me about the last time you placed a produce order.
> *Why: specific situation, not "how do you usually order" (Rule 4).*
>
> **Marisol:** Monday night. I looked in the walk-in, guessed how busy the week would be, and texted my guy. I always add extra cilantro and avocados because running out at lunch is the worst.
>
> **Priya:** Why is running out the worst?
> *Why: follows up on her exact words to get motivation (Rules 2 and 5).*
>
> **Marisol:** If I 86 guacamole on a Friday, people leave bad reviews. Throwing out avocados costs me less than that.
>
> **Priya:** When was the last time you threw some out?
> *Why: turns a general statement into a specific, checkable event.*
>
> **Marisol:** Last week, honestly. Half a case went brown. Maybe forty bucks. That happens most weeks with something.
>
> **Priya:** What have you tried to deal with it?
> *Why: alternatives (Rule 8).*
>
> **Marisol:** I tried a spreadsheet my nephew made. Gave up after two weeks; I don't have time at 10 p.m. to type in counts.
>
> **Priya:** What impact does that weekly waste have on the business?
> *Why: urgency, asked neutrally using her framing.*
>
> **Marisol:** It's annoying, but it's not what keeps me up. Labor is. If I could fix scheduling, that'd matter way more.
> *A twist. Priya follows it instead of steering back.*
>
> **Priya:** Tell me more about scheduling. What happened the last time it went wrong?

**Priya's evidence record (excerpt):**

```
Interviewee: owner-operator, 40-seat taqueria     Profile match: Yes
Exact quotes: "I always add extra cilantro and avocados because running out at
  lunch is the worst." / "Throwing out avocados costs me less than that." /
  "It's not what keeps me up. Labor is."
Situation and problem: Monday order by gut feel; half a case of avocados wasted
  last week (~$40); waste is weekly.
Current alternative: over-ordering on purpose; abandoned spreadsheet (no time).
Evidence of urgency: Low for produce waste (seen as cost of not running out);
  high for labor scheduling.
Implications: A1 supported. A2 partly contradicted: waste is noticed but treated
  as acceptable insurance. A3 supported (spreadsheet abandoned).
Surprises: labor scheduling is the bigger pain.
```

**Why this is the model:** every question is open-ended and non-leading; she anchors on the last order, follows up on Marisol's own words, never mentions a product, and lets the conversation turn. The record keeps verbatim quotes and is honest that A2 weakened. If several more owners repeat "waste is insurance, labor is the real problem," the pattern points to a **Pivot** toward a different problem, not a Proceed.

### Negative Example: An Awful Interview

**Interviewee:** Dan, owner of a neighborhood pizza place.

> **Priya:** Hi! I'm building an AI-driven predictive par-level optimization platform for restaurants. Can I ask you a few questions?
> *Pitches the product in the first line and uses jargon ("predictive par-level optimization") (Rules 3 and 9). Dan now knows what she wants to hear.*
>
> **Dan:** Uh, sure.
>
> **Priya:** Do you waste a lot of produce?
> *Yes/no and leading; "a lot" suggests the answer (Rules 1 and 3).*
>
> **Dan:** I mean, some, I guess.
>
> **Priya:** How do you usually handle ordering?
> *Asks for a generality, not a specific recent order (Rule 4).*
>
> **Dan:** Usually I just know what we need.
>
> **Priya:** Right, but gut feel isn't really a reliable way to run a business, is it?
> *Judges him and leads him (Rule 6). He is now defensive.*
>
> **Dan:** It's worked for twelve years.
>
> **Priya:** Okay, next question. Don't you think a tool that predicts your orders automatically would save you a ton of money?
> *Ignores his answer to read the next scripted question (Rules 5 and 10), and asks the textbook leading question (Rule 3).*
>
> **Dan:** Sure, that sounds great.
>
> **Priya:** Awesome! Would you pay $99 a month for it?
> *Hypothetical, yes/no, and asked after leading him; polite agreement is guaranteed.*
>
> **Dan:** Yeah, probably.

**Priya's "evidence record":** "Dan loves the idea, would pay $99/mo. Proceed!"

**Why this fails:** no honest intro or "not selling"; jargon; yes/no and leading questions; generalities instead of a specific order; judgment that made him defensive; no follow-up on "It's worked for twelve years" (the most interesting thing he said); no question about alternatives; no exact quotes about a real situation; no profile, urgency, or assumption check. The "Proceed" rests on one politely agreeable comment that she created herself. Nothing here is evidence.
