# Business Model

You are an expert in business model design, analysis, and risk. Your goal is to turn a product, an idea, or an existing company into a complete, internally consistent, honestly assessed business model (one that can deliver its value **repeatedly and profitably**) and to show exactly which assumptions must be tested before real money is put at risk.

## Before You Start

Business models get built, audited, optimized, revised, compared, and pivoted. Establish which of these you are doing, then gather:

1. **Situation**: a new idea, an existing business to audit or optimize, a model to revise or pivot, candidate models to compare, or someone else's model to analyze (a competitor, an acquisition, an investment).
2. **Problem and customer**: who has the problem and how painful it is (→ [problem-statement](../problem-statement/SKILL.md)); which segments you serve (→ [customer-segmentation](../customer-segmentation/SKILL.md)).
3. **Offer**: the value proposition and why customers choose it over alternatives (→ [value-proposition](../value-proposition/SKILL.md)).
4. **What already exists**: a current canvas, revenue and cost figures, contracts, customer evidence, competitor data.
5. **Constraints**: what the founder can afford to lose in time, money, reputation, and effort (see [Affordable Loss](#affordable-loss)).

If key context is missing, ask the one to three questions that matter most. If the user wants speed, proceed and tag every guess as **Assumed** so nothing guessed is passed off as fact.

**Quality rule: always aim for perfection in your own work.** The anti-perfectionism in this skill ("test small," "start with a reversible experiment," the new playbook's iterate-and-learn) describes how the *business* should take risk in the real world. For you, the agent, it is inverted: never ship a "good enough" draft, never skip a block, never leave a guess untagged. Every canvas, assessment, and recommendation you produce is complete, internally consistent, checked block against block, and the best version you can make.

## Why a Value Proposition Is Not Enough

A **value proposition is required, but it will not guarantee success.** What else is needed is **a way to deliver your product or service repeatedly and profitably.** Your business model is what allows you to do this.

The classic failure pattern:
1. An entrepreneur builds an innovative product.
2. The product does not translate into a successful business.
3. The entrepreneur is stumped about **why** it did not work.

To succeed, a venture must identify the right:
- **Customer Segments** (target customers)
- **Value Proposition** (why us)
- **Delivery channel(s)**
- **Revenue strategy / profit formula**
- **Key activities / resources**
- **Branding / messaging**

In short: the venture must build a proper business model.

## Business Model — Summary

A **business model**:
- **Describes the rationale of how an organization creates, delivers, and captures value.**
- **Functions as the blueprint to be implemented through organizational structures, processes, and systems.**
- **Simply put: the way a company generates revenue and makes a profit from company operations.**

The Business Model Canvas is the tool for **mapping how a business creates, delivers, and captures value**:

| Verb | What it means | Where it lives on the canvas |
|---|---|---|
| **Create** | Produce the solution | Key Activities, Key Resources, Key Partners (the backstage) feeding the Value Proposition |
| **Deliver** | Get it to and keep it with customers | Channels, Customer Relationships, Customer Segments (the front stage) |
| **Capture** | Keep some of the value as profit | Revenue Streams minus Cost Structure |

### Two Real Business Models in One Glance

Two global leaders, two very different blueprints:

**Nike: own the differentiation, outsource the rest.** Nike holds its leadership position in the highly competitive sportswear equipment and apparel market with a business model design that focuses on:
- Core activities like design, development, and marketing
- Outsourcing non-core activities like manufacturing to contract manufacturers
- Building a truly global brand

Nike **does not own factories** and **does not "make" shoes, clothes, or equipment.** It knows that to create customer value it must concentrate resources on differentiation (the VP): product design, athlete/consumer insight, brand, marketing, and distribution.

**AB InBev: acquire, scale, discipline.** AB InBev describes its business model as "designed to deliver long-term, sustainable value through top-line growth and expanding profitability," resting on six pillars: **Dream-People-Culture, Brand portfolio, Point of connection, Leading market positions, Strategy, Financial discipline.** It creates value three ways:
- Acquire proven brands and market positions
- Use global scale and distribution
- Improve profitability through operational discipline

Scale on the slides: around 200,000 colleagues in over 50 countries, over 500 beer brands, beer sold in more than 150 countries, and 7 of the BrandZ Top Ten (2017 BrandZ Top 100 Most Valuable Global Brands). The acquisition engine:

| Year | Acquisition / deal | Amount paid |
|---|---|---|
| 2004 | AmBev (merger with Interbrew) | Formed InBev |
| 2008 | Anheuser-Busch | $52 billion |
| 2012 | Cervecería Nacional Dominicana | $2.4 billion |
| 2013 | Grupo Modelo | $20.1 billion |
| 2014 | Oriental Brewery (South Korea) | $5.8 billion |
| 2014 | Siping Ginsber (China) | $630 million |
| 2016 | SABMiller | $100–107 billion |
| 2019 | Carlton & United Breweries (Australia; sold to Asahi) | $11–16 billion |

**Lesson:** the product category (sportswear, beer) does not dictate the model. The choices about what to own, what to do yourself, what to outsource, and how to grow do.

## Business Model Template — Text Version

Use this one-paragraph template to state any business model fast, before or alongside the canvas:

```text
[Product/Service] at center
  - targeting [Customer Segments]
  - delivering [Value Proposition]
  - through [Channels]
  - communicated via [Branding/Messaging]
  - generating [Revenue/Profit]

Big Question: Is the value created worth the price charged? (revenue/profit formula)
```

**The Big Question** is the test of every business model: **is the value created worth the price charged?** If customers will not pay a price that covers the cost of creating and delivering that value, the model fails no matter how good the product is.

**Filled example (Nike, from the slides):**

```text
[Athletic footwear, apparel, and equipment] at center
  - targeting [footwear, apparel, and equipment customers across running,
    basketball, football, training, action sports, sportswear, and golf]
  - delivering [differentiated product design built on athlete/consumer insight]
  - through [direct: nike.com, Nike stores; dealer networks: footwear, sporting
    goods, athletic specialty, and department stores; skate, tennis, and golf shops]
  - communicated via [a truly global brand and heavy marketing (demand creation)]
  - generating [product revenue: footwear 68%, apparel 28%, equipment 4% (FY2024)]

Big Question: the brand premium is what lets the value created exceed the price
of contract-manufactured goods.
```

### How the Text Version Maps to the Canvas

| Text template slot | Canvas block(s) |
|---|---|
| [Product/Service] at center | Value Propositions (what is offered) |
| targeting [Customer Segments] | Customer Segments |
| delivering [Value Proposition] | Value Propositions |
| through [Channels] | Channels |
| communicated via [Branding/Messaging] | Channels (awareness and evaluation phases) and Customer Relationships; the brand itself is an intellectual Key Resource |
| generating [Revenue/Profit] | Revenue Streams minus Cost Structure |
| *(not in the text version)* | Key Activities, Key Resources, Key Partners |

The text version is mostly front stage. It hides the backstage (what you must do, own, and partner for), which is where most cost and most execution risk sit. Use it for a fast statement; use the canvas for the full picture.

## Determine Your Model: Three Questions

Think about three questions. Each one points to a family of models.

| Question | Model family | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **How are you charging: one-time or recurring fees?** | Revenue Stream Models | Bottled water (one-time), Netflix (recurring) |
| **Are you connecting different parties?** | Platform / Marketplace Models | Uber, Etsy |
| **What do you own that creates value?** | Asset / IP / Distribution Models | Disney |

### Mix and Match: One Business, Several Models

These models are **building blocks, not exclusive choices.** A strong business usually combines several revenue models, for example:
- A direct-sales core plus a subscription for consumables (razor and blade).
- A free tier funded by advertising plus a paid premium tier (freemium).
- Products sold through distribution partners plus licensing of the brand.
- A marketplace commission plus an affiliate income stream.

When you design or audit a model, list **every** way the business makes money and ask which additional models from the lists below could strengthen it (more recurring revenue, earlier cash, others doing the work).

## List of Business Models

### Revenue Stream Models — How Businesses Charge for Value

| Model | How it makes money | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Subscription** | Recurring fees paid for access to services/products (monthly, quarterly, annually) | Netflix, Dollar Shave Club, Blue Apron |
| **Freemium** | Offer basic services for free, then charge a premium for extra add-ons | Spotify, Zoom |
| **Advertising** | Provide free products or services to users; generate revenue by displaying advertisements | Visithoustontexas.com |
| **Direct Sales** | Sell to customers online without retail partners | Dell |
| **Razor and Blade** | No/low profit on the main component; profit via ongoing purchases | Gillette, Keurig, inkjet printers |

### Platform and Marketplace Models — Connecting Buyers, Sellers, and Users

| Model | How it makes money | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Marketplace** | Connect buyers and sellers via a platform; earn revenue via transaction fees or commissions | Etsy, Amazon |
| **Platform** | Provide technological infrastructure that enables users to connect, interact, and exchange value | Uber, Airbnb |
| **On-Demand** | Provide products or services to customers on demand through a digital platform or app | UberEATS, DoorDash, Drizly |
| **Affiliate Marketing** | Promote/drive sales (e.g., social media) for other companies' products/services; earn commissions or referral fees | Rakuten, Amazon |
| **Network Marketing** | Build a sales organization, recruit others, and earn commissions from the network | Amway, Mary Kay, Avon |

Platforms and marketplaces serve two or more groups that depend on each other, so map each side and its role (→ [customer-ecosystem](../customer-ecosystem/SKILL.md)).

### Asset, IP, and Distribution Models — Leveraging Resources and Rights

| Model | How it makes money | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Asset Sale** | Sell ownership or usage rights of physical or digital assets to customers | Airbnb, Turo, Freedom Boat Club |
| **Licensing** | License IP (trademarks, patents, etc.) to other businesses to use | Walt Disney, Marvel |
| **Franchise** | Develop a business model/brand, allow others to use it, collect royalties | Subway, Dunkin' Donuts |
| **Manufacturer / Supplier** | Manufacture and supply products; generate revenue through sales by distribution partners | Intel, Firerock, Ford |
| **Profit Sharing** | Distribute a portion of profits among partners/stakeholders on a predetermined basis | Unsellable Houses, a plumbing company |

### Choosing Among Candidate Models

The same opportunity can be run as very different businesses. Slide scenario: driving around town, you notice many old apartment buildings, which are very inefficient with water, and you want to help owners gain efficiencies (save water and money). Four possible business models come to mind:

| Candidate | Model | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **Matchmaker** | Platform / marketplace | A website linking apartment owners with resources to help them modernize |
| **Consultant** | Service fee | You (or people you hire) personally work with owners and manage the resources brought in, for a fee |
| **Plumbing company** | Direct service sales | Create a plumbing company and target these owners for services |
| **Shared Savings** | Profit sharing | Modernize the plumbing and take a portion of the savings as payment |

**Which business model is better? Discuss, ask questions, decide.** Do not pick by gut. For each candidate, answer:
1. What customer problem is being solved? (→ [problem-statement](../problem-statement/SKILL.md))
2. How will you make money?
3. Why should this customer choose you rather than an alternative? (→ [value-proposition](../value-proposition/SKILL.md))
4. Which model type fits best? Why?

Then score every candidate on the [Business Model Assessment](#business-model-assessment) and compare them side by side. Generate more candidates than feel necessary before choosing (→ [creative-ideation](../creative-ideation/SKILL.md)).

## The Business Model Canvas

The canvas is the best framework for a business model: **one page, the whole business at a glance.**

### The Nine Building Blocks in Four Areas

| Area | Blocks | Question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| **Customers** | Customer Segments, Customer Relationships, Channels | Who do we serve and how do we reach them? |
| **Offer** | Value Proposition | What value do we deliver? |
| **Infrastructure** | Key Activities, Key Resources, Key Partnerships | How do we produce it? |
| **Financial viability** | Revenue Streams, Cost Structure | Does it make money? |

### The Canvas Layout

Standard layout. Header fields: **Designed for**, **Designed by**, **Date**, **Version**.

```text
+---------------+---------------+---------------+---------------+---------------+
| Key Partners  | Key           | Value         | Customer      | Customer      |
|               | Activities    | Propositions  | Relationships | Segments      |
|               +---------------+               +---------------+               |
|               | Key           |               | Channels      |               |
|               | Resources     |               |               |               |
+---------------+---------------+-------+-------+---------------+---------------+
| Cost Structure                        | Revenue Streams                       |
+---------------------------------------+---------------------------------------+
```

### Backstage, Front Stage, and Where They Meet

| Zone | Blocks | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **All about the customer: front stage** | Customer Segments, Customer Relationships, Channels, Revenue Streams | How we interact with customers |
| **All about the solution: backstage** | Key Partners, Key Activities, Key Resources, Cost Structure | How we produce our solution |
| **Where they meet** | Value Proposition | The value proposition connects them |

Read the canvas right-to-left for **desirability** (who, why, how reached, what they pay) and left-to-right for **feasibility and cost** (what we must do, own, and partner for, and what it costs). Every backstage entry should exist because the value proposition or a front-stage block needs it.

### A Canvas Is a Set of Assumptions

**Filling in a box does not prove it is true.** Tag every entry with one of three statuses:

| Status | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Assumed** | Our best guess, written down so it can be tested |
| **Supported by evidence** | Backed by something observed or measured |
| **Needs testing** | Could sink the business if wrong — test it first |

Moving entries from Assumed to Supported by evidence is customer validation work (→ [customer-validation](../customer-validation/SKILL.md)), most often through customer interviews (→ [customer-interview](../customer-interview/SKILL.md)).

## The Nine Blocks in Detail

Fill the blocks in this order. It follows the logic of the business: customer first, then offer, then how you reach, keep, and earn from them, then what it takes to produce it, then what it costs.

### 1. Customer Segments

**The different groups of people your company aims to reach and serve.** On the canvas, list each segment separately; segment types (mass market, niche, segmented, diversified, multi-sided platform) and how to choose them are covered in the owner skill (→ [customer-segmentation](../customer-segmentation/SKILL.md)).

Canvas-specific rules:
- If two groups need **different channels, different relationships, different economics, or pay differently**, they are separate segments and get separate entries, because they drive different entries in every other block.
- For platforms and marketplaces, every side is a segment (Airbnb's travelers *and* hosts), and the sides depend on each other (→ [customer-ecosystem](../customer-ecosystem/SKILL.md)).
- Nike illustrates a multi-segment structure: Footwear, Apparel, Equipment, and Global Brands (Jordan Brand, Hurley, Converse), each broken into running, basketball, football, men's and women's training, action sports, sportswear, golf, and equipment lines.

### 2. Value Propositions

**The products/services that create value for your customer segments, plus the reasons these customers turn to your company over another.** Value proposition types, frameworks, and differentiation are covered in the owner skill (→ [value-proposition](../value-proposition/SKILL.md)).

Canvas-specific rules:
- Write one value proposition per segment if segments value different things.
- The VP is **where front stage and backstage meet**: every key activity and key resource should trace to it.
- Nike concentrates its resources on exactly the activities that create its differentiation: product design, athlete/consumer insight, brand, marketing, distribution.

### 3. Channels

**How you reach your customers and communicate with them to deliver your value proposition.**

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct** | You own the customer contact | Sales team, own stores, online sales platform, events and trade shows, email marketing, social media, mobile apps |
| **Indirect** | Someone else carries your offer | Partner stores, wholesalers, distributors, content marketing, affiliates (influencers), partnerships/alliances, social media, mobile apps |

Social media and mobile apps can be either: direct when you run them, indirect when a partner or influencer does.

**Channel functions:** raising awareness, helping prospects evaluate your value proposition, providing customer support, and more. Design a channel for every phase:

| Phase | Awareness | Evaluation | Purchase | Delivery | After sales |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Question** | How do we raise awareness of our products and services? | How do we help customers evaluate our value proposition? | How do we let customers purchase our products and services? | How do we deliver our value proposition? | How do we provide post-purchase support? |

Example: seeing a Nike ad this week is the awareness phase; researching shoes online before buying is the evaluation phase.

**Nike's channels:**
- **Direct:** online sales (nike.com), Nike factory stores (plus Nike brand and category experience stores)
- **Dealer networks:** footwear stores, sporting goods stores, athletic specialty stores, department stores, skate, tennis, and golf shops, licenses

**Nike direct vs. wholesale** (revenue, $ millions, Statista 2024):

| Year | Sales to wholesale customers | Sales through NIKE Direct |
|---|---|---|
| 2016 | 22,577 | 7,857 |
| 2018 | 23,969 | 10,428 |
| 2020 | 23,156 | 12,382 |
| 2022 | 25,608 | 18,726 |
| 2024 | 27,758 | 21,519 |

Direct grew from about 26% to about 44% of these two channels combined between 2016 and 2024, while wholesale stayed the larger channel. Read this alongside the [Nike $100 shoe cost structure](#9-cost-structure), where the retailer margin is $50 of a $100 shoe: every sale moved from wholesale to direct keeps more of that margin in-house. Channel choice is a revenue and cost decision, not only a reach decision.

### 4. Customer Relationships

**How your company finds, acquires, and builds relationships with customers.**

- **Customer acquisition:** online/offline ads, word-of-mouth
- **Customer retention:** newsletters, targeted promotions

| Relationship type | Example |
|---|---|
| **Personal assistance** | Financial advisor |
| **Automated services** | Chatbots |
| **Communities and co-creation** | LEGO |
| **Long-term engagement** | Starbucks loyalty |
| **Self-service** | Self-checkout stores, online banking |

Choose the relationship type the segment's economics can afford: personal assistance is costly per customer; automated and self-service relationships scale cheaply (Spirit Airlines runs **automated, impersonal** relationships because its value proposition is cheap flights).

### 5. Revenue Streams

**The cash generated from each customer segment.** Each segment should have at least one revenue entry; a segment with no revenue line must be justified (for example, the free side of a platform).

| Revenue mechanism | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Asset sale** | Selling ownership rights to a physical product | Amazon |
| **Usage fee** | Charged by the use of a particular service | Phone subscription |
| **Subscription fee** | Selling access to a service | Netflix |
| **Renting** | The right to use an asset for a fixed period, for a fee | Avis |
| **Brokerage fee (commission)** | Intermediation services on behalf of two parties | Airbnb |

These mechanisms are how the [business model types](#list-of-business-models) actually charge. Note for each stream: one-time or recurring, and when the cash arrives relative to when you spend (this drives the assessment).

Example revenue mix: Nike's FY2024 revenue share by product category was footwear 68%, apparel 28%, equipment 4% (Statista 2024). Spirit Airlines earns from **tickets and fees**.

### 6. Key Resources

**The assets required to make your business work.**

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Physical** | Facilities, buildings, vehicles, distribution networks |
| **Intellectual** | Brands, knowledge, copyrights, databases |
| **Human** | Sales force |
| **Financial** | Cash, lines of credit, stock option pools |

Nike's key resources: **manufacturing, brand, supply chain, patents and other IP.** Nike does not own its factories, so its manufacturing capacity is reached through contract-manufacturer partners. A key resource does not have to be owned; it has to be reliably available.

Spirit Airlines' key resources: a **single aircraft model** and **cheap airports**, both of which directly enable its cheap-flight value proposition.

### 7. Key Activities

**The most important things your company must do to make its business model work.**

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Production** | Design, make, and produce a product | Nike: R&D, manufacturing and distribution |
| **Problem solving** | New solutions or help | The Consultant candidate in the water-efficiency scenario |
| **Platform / network** | Run and grow the platform or network | Amazon, Visa |

**Resources are what we need; activities are what we must do.** Do not list the same thing in both blocks: "brand" is a resource, "demand creation" is the activity that builds it.

Nike's key activities: **R&D, manufacturing and distribution, demand creation.** Spirit's: **quick turnarounds**.

### 8. Key Partners

**Who you partner with to make your business work:**
- Suppliers
- Strategic alliances
- Joint ventures

The test question: **Who outside the business supplies a critical resource or performs a critical activity?**

Nike shows how partners carry both cost and revenue (slide "How NIKE makes money?"):

| Partner | What flows to Nike | What flows from Nike (cost) |
|---|---|---|
| **Contract manufacturers** | Finished goods ready for retail and/or public consumption | Finished-goods payments, technology transfer |
| **Influencers** | Brand premium, goodwill | Investments in building a sustainable and profitable business model |
| **Dealers** | Revenue from buying the products and keeping inventories | Logistics costs, including shipping and handling |
| **Customer segments (direct)** | Revenue for products purchased via experience stores, nike.com, and factory stores | (served directly) |

Dealers in turn sell and promote the products to customer segments (footwear, apparel, equipment) and collect payment. Mapping partners this way shows each one's role in the ecosystem (→ [customer-ecosystem](../customer-ecosystem/SKILL.md)).

Spirit Airlines' key partners: **car rental, hotel, insurance**.

### 9. Cost Structure

**All the costs incurred to operate your business.**

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Cost-driven** | Low-price value, maximum automation | Budget airlines such as Spirit |
| **Value-driven** | Added-value focused | Luxury hotels |
| **Fixed costs** | Do not change with volume | Salaries and rents |
| **Variable costs** | Vary with the volume of goods produced | Nike's $22 cost to produce each shoe (below) |

**Cost structure questions:**
- What are the most important costs inherent in our business?
- Which key resources are the most expensive?
- Which key activities are the most expensive?

**The financial anatomy of a $100 Nike shoe** (SoleReview.com; based on overall cost of sales per Nike's 2015 income statement; freight, insurance, and duties are assumptions; taxes calculated on gross income, which is 7% of the retail price):

| Component | Amount |
|---|---|
| Cost to produce | $22 |
| Freight, insurance, customs duty | $5 |
| Marketing | $5 |
| Other expenses and overheads | $11 |
| Income taxes | $2 |
| **Net profit (Nike)** | **$5** |
| Retailer margin | $50 |

Nike's profit on a $100 shoe is $5. The biggest single slice goes to the channel, not to production, which is why channel design belongs in the same conversation as cost structure.

Spirit Airlines' costs: **maintenance, training, airports, call centers**, which is a cost-driven structure aligned with its "cheap flights, no frills" value proposition.

## Reading a Canvas as a System: Spirit Airlines

A good canvas is not nine lists; it is one logic. Spirit Airlines on the slides:

| Block | Entries |
|---|---|
| Key Partners | Car rental, hotel, insurance |
| Key Activities | Quick turnarounds |
| Key Resources | Single aircraft model, cheap airports |
| Value Proposition | Cheap flights, no frills |
| Customer Relationships | Automated, impersonal |
| Channels | Call centers, www |
| Customer Segments | Budget travelers |
| Cost Structure | Maintenance, training, airports, call centers |
| Revenue Streams | Tickets, fees |

The slide draws arrows through the canvas: quick turnarounds and the single-aircraft, cheap-airport resources feed the cheap-flights value proposition; activities and resources flow down into specific cost lines (maintenance, airports); budget travelers flow down into tickets and fees. **Everything points at the value proposition, and every cost traces to an activity or resource.** Use the same check on any canvas:
- Every key activity and key resource supports the VP or a front-stage block.
- Every cost line traces to a key activity, key resource, or key partner.
- Every customer segment has a channel, a relationship, and a revenue stream.
- The cost structure type (cost-driven or value-driven) matches the VP.

## The Ripple Test: What Else Changes?

Changing one block changes others. Slide problem: **a bakery shifts from walk-in purchases to weekly office deliveries.** Keep the product similar, but change the customer and delivery model, then ask: **which resources, activities, relationships, revenue streams, or costs must change?**

Worked through: Customer Segments move from walk-in individuals to offices; Channels move from the storefront to scheduled delivery; Customer Relationships move from one-off transactions to a recurring account; Revenue Streams move from per-item sales toward a weekly order or subscription; Key Activities add order scheduling and delivery; Key Resources add a delivery vehicle; Cost Structure adds delivery cost. The product barely moved, yet most of the canvas did.

Run the ripple test on every proposed change, pivot, or optimization before committing to it.

## Business Model Assessment

Score the model on seven factors, each from **0 to 10**. The left anchor is 0, the right anchor is 10.

| Factor | 0 = | 10 = |
|---|---|---|
| **Switching Costs** | Nothing keeps my customers from leaving me | My customers are locked in for several years |
| **Recurring Revenues** | 100% of sales are transactional | 100% of sales lead to recurring revenues |
| **Earning vs. Spending** | I incur 100% of my COGS before earning revenue | I earn 100% of my revenue before incurring COGS |
| **Game-Changing Cost Structure** | My cost structure is 30% higher than my competitors | My cost structure is 30% lower than my competitors |
| **Others Who Do the Work** | I incur costs for all value created in my business model | Value is created in the business for free by external parties |
| **Scalability** | Growing the business model requires substantial resources and effort | My business model has virtually no limits to growth |
| **Protection from Competition** | I am vulnerable to competition | I am not vulnerable to competition |

### How to Use It

- **Score with evidence.** Every score cites a fact from the canvas or from research. A score with no evidence behind it is itself an Assumed entry.
- **The slides give no pass mark: use the scores to compare candidate models and to find the weakest factor to redesign.**
- **Compare candidates side by side** (see [Choosing Among Candidate Models](#choosing-among-candidate-models)). The same opportunity scores very differently as a matchmaker, a consultancy, a service company, or a shared-savings model.
- **Re-score after every revision** and keep the versions; the trend matters more than the absolute number.
- **Cost structure and protection scores need competitor data.** You cannot claim "30% lower than competitors" or "not vulnerable" without a competitive analysis (→ [market-research](../market-research/SKILL.md)).

## Risk: Calculated vs. Reckless

Every business model is a bet. **Calculated risk taking** is a fundamental entrepreneurial behavior; reckless risk is how good ideas die. The difference is not how big the bet is but how it is structured.

### Affordable Loss

**Before acting (starting), ask: what can I afford to lose in time, money, reputation, and effort in order to learn?**

Set the affordable loss first, then design the first version of the model so that the worst case stays inside it.

### Smart Risk vs. Reckless Risk

| | **Smart risk** | **Reckless risk** |
|---|---|---|
| **Character** | Calculated; informed by research; knowledgeable (mentors) | Driven by ego; overconfident; gambling mentality |
| **Result** | Manages the downside (affordable loss); reversible / learnable; evidence-based | Downside is catastrophic; succeed or lose everything |

"Informed by research" means real evidence about customers, competitors, and market size, not a hopeful estimate (→ [market-research](../market-research/SKILL.md)).

### Reckless: WeWork

**Overconfidence**
- "We will be the world's largest real estate company" (founder Adam Neumann)
- A $3 trillion market opportunity (wildly overestimated)
- Valued at $47 billion in 2019 (never profitable)

**Illusion of control**
- **Asset vs. liability mismatch (business model):** WeWork signed long-term leases (10–15 years) and rented short-term to tenants (month-to-month).

**Logic:** move fast, scale fast, profitability will follow.

**Result:** lost money on every sublease in 2019 (earned less from tenants than it owed landlords).

**The collapse:** 2019 IPO due diligence exposed the (<15%) gross margin loss to investors; the valuation plummeted from $47B to $10B in weeks; SoftBank (the largest investor) bailed it out; the founder (Neumann) was ousted; the company filed for bankruptcy.

**Lesson: overconfidence + business model + weak governance = catastrophe.**

In assessment terms: Earning vs. Spending near 0 (long fixed commitments made before revenue), Switching Costs near 0 (month-to-month tenants), and a cost structure that exceeded revenue per unit. The canvas would have shown the mismatch: the Cost Structure was fixed and long-term while the Revenue Streams were cancellable monthly.

### Smart: Airbnb

**Managing downside while learning.** Founded in the same era as WeWork:
- Started with a simple, reversible plan: can we let strangers stay in homes?
- If it failed, the downside was manageable: lose the app, not $1B in leases.

**Business model**
- Focused and clear: peer-to-peer short-term stays
- Hosts own the property; Airbnb just takes a commission
- Gross margin 70%+ (money per transaction)

**Lesson: reversible decisions + strong unit economics + willingness to adapt = success.**

In assessment terms: Others Who Do the Work is high (hosts supply the key physical resource), and Earning vs. Spending is strong (commission is earned per transaction rather than committed in advance).

### Four Ways to Improve Entrepreneurial Judgment

1. **Write down your key assumptions before acting.** (The status-tagged canvas does this.)
2. **Seek evidence that could prove you wrong.**
3. **Ask informed outsiders to challenge your plan.**
4. **Start with a small, reversible experiment.**

### Risk Check for Any Business Model

Run this on every canvas before recommending action:

```text

```

## Old Playbook vs. New Playbook

From John Hagel III, "We Need to Expand Our Definition of Entrepreneurship."

| | **Old playbook** | **New playbook** |
|---|---|---|
| **Sequence** | Standardize → Scale (mass production) → Dominant | Identify a *niche* that technology makes accessible and profitable |
| **Moves** | Identify a task (standardize); organize it for repeatable efficiency (scale); win the whole market (dominate) | Find a specific customer problem; create a focused solution; test and adapt |
| **Examples / results** | Carnegie: US Steel; Rockefeller: Standard Oil (Exxon, Mobil, Chevron); Edison: GE. Massive wealth, a middle class, consolidation to a few giants | Content delivered how many ways; transportation delivered how many ways |
| **Goal** | "Be the biggest" | Join the entrepreneurial society; small teams, specific customers (you do not have to conquer the world); not where a few giants win but where many niche founders thrive |

**The shift:** from a manufacturing economy to a digital/service economy. Return on assets is down 75% since 1965 (Deloitte study of 20,000 companies); in many industries, owning more physical assets no longer guarantees superior returns; digital tools and platforms let smaller firms reach specialized markets. Result: technology makes opportunities accessible at a rapid rate.

**What it means for model design:**
- A model does not need to own everything to win. Nike outsources manufacturing; Airbnb's hosts own the property.
- A focused model serving a specific segment well is a legitimate end state, not a failure to scale. Pick the niche deliberately (→ [customer-segmentation](../customer-segmentation/SKILL.md)) and confirm it is a real opportunity (→ [opportunity-id](../opportunity-id/SKILL.md)).
- "Test and adapt" is built into the new playbook: treat the canvas as versioned assumptions, not a fixed plan.

## Growth Mindset and Open-Mindedness

Every canvas will have entries that the evidence contradicts. What the founder does next depends on mindset: one mindset defends the old model or quits, the other learns and revises it.

### Fixed vs. Growth Mindset

| | **Trapped: fixed mindset** | **Free: growth mindset** |
|---|---|---|
| **After a test goes badly** | "My first product/idea/test failed." → "I'm not cut out for this (entrepreneurship)." | "My product/idea/test did not support my assumptions." → "What did I learn?" "What will I try next?" |
| **Thinking style** | Trait-based thinking | Process-based thinking |
| **Challenges** | Avoids challenges | Embraces challenges |
| **Setbacks** | Gives up easily | Persists |
| **Feedback** | Ignores feedback | Learns from feedback |
| **Others' success** | Feels threatened by others' success | Gets inspired by others' success |

The key reframe: **a test did not "fail"; it did not support an assumption.** That treats the result as information about a canvas entry, not as a judgment of the founder. In canvas terms, an entry the evidence contradicts gets revised and re-tagged, not defended.

### Airbnb: Customer Observations Changed the Approach

**2008–2009:** the founders pitched to investors and got rejected *repeatedly*.
- **Problem:** the concept seemed too radical. People letting strangers stay in their homes? Investors thought it was insane.
- **Result:** nearly zero bookings; the business was failing.

Two possible responses:

| **Fixed mindset** | **Growth mindset** |
|---|---|
| Investors are wrong; they do not get it | Go and stay with hosts/users |
| The market doesn't understand our vision | Listen to why bookings are not happening |
| People will never trust staying with strangers | Identified: photos of host places vs. actual places were not accurate (blurry, bad lighting) |
| We tried; it's not working; let's quit | |

**Mindset in action:**
- **Strategy:** hire a photographer to take professional photos of every listing.
- **Result:** better photos, more bookings. Bookings skyrocketed.
- **Lesson:** the problem wasn't the idea; it was the *execution*.
- **What to do:** don't blame the market. Ask: what can *we* do **differently**?

**Fail → Analyze → Learn → Adapt, Persist, or Stop.**

Read on the canvas, the fix sat in the evaluation phase of Channels (listing photos are how guests evaluate a stay) and in a new key activity (professional photography). The segments, the value proposition, and the commission revenue stream did not change. Before changing the whole model, find the block that is actually failing; listening to customers is how you find it (→ [customer-interview](../customer-interview/SKILL.md)).

**Open-mindedness in practice:** every canvas entry, including the founder's favorite, is open to revision when the evidence moves. Pair this with the judgment habits above: seek evidence that could prove you wrong, and ask informed outsiders to challenge the plan.

"Fail" and "learn" here describe how the founder treats real-world tests. They are never a standard for your own work as the agent (see the Quality rule).

## Openness to Pivoting

Willingness to adapt is one of the three parts of Airbnb's success formula: **reversible decisions + strong unit economics + willingness to adapt.** Pivoting is not a confession of failure; it is the planned exit from a learning loop.

### The Entrepreneurial Learning Cycle

**Observe a problem → Make an assumption → Run a small test → Learn from evidence → Adapt, Persist, or Stop.** Learning from evidence loops back to making a new assumption until the evidence is strong enough to adapt, persist, or stop.

| Step | Example from the slides |
|---|---|
| **Problem** | Students struggle to find quiet study space after 7 p.m. (→ [problem-statement](../problem-statement/SKILL.md)) |
| **Assumption** | Students would pay for a real-time study-space locator |
| **Test** | Interview 10 students and post a simple prototype or sign-up page (→ [customer-interview](../customer-interview/SKILL.md)) |
| **Learn** | Do they experience the problem? Would they use or pay for the solution? |

On a business model, each loop targets one [T] entry: the assumption is the entry, the test is the cheapest way to get evidence on it (→ [customer-validation](../customer-validation/SKILL.md)), and the result moves it to [E], revises it, or removes it.

### Adapt, Persist, or Stop

The deck ends every loop with three exits: **adapt, persist, or stop.** Before acting, answer the checklist question: **"Adapt based on evidence: what would make you persist, pivot, or stop?"** Write those criteria down before the test runs, so the evidence makes the call, not ego or sunk cost.

The decision itself (Proceed / Pivot / Punt) is owned by problem-statement (→ [problem-statement](../problem-statement/SKILL.md)). This skill adds what a pivot means for the model:
- **Locate the failure first.** Airbnb's problem was execution in one block, not the idea. Fix the failing block before replacing the model.
- **Ripple-test every pivot.** A change to one block moves others (see [The Ripple Test](#the-ripple-test-what-else-changes)).
- **Keep pivots reversible and inside the affordable loss** (see [Risk: Calculated vs. Reckless](#risk-calculated-vs-reckless)).
- **Version the canvas.** A pivot is a new canvas version, re-scored on the assessment, with its own [T] entries.

**Entrepreneurs do not wait for certainty. They identify problems, test assumptions, manage downside, learn from feedback, and adapt.**

The founder's small tests and quick pivots never lower the bar for your own analysis: every revised canvas you produce is complete and checked block against block.

## Workflow

Use this for any job: designing, auditing, optimizing, revising, comparing, or pivoting.

1. **Anchor.** Confirm the problem (→ [problem-statement](../problem-statement/SKILL.md)), the segments (→ [customer-segmentation](../customer-segmentation/SKILL.md)), and the value proposition (→ [value-proposition](../value-proposition/SKILL.md)). Remember the order: **differentiated customer value (VP) first, then business-model design.**
2. **State it in one paragraph** with the [text template](#business-model-template--text-version) and answer the Big Question.
3. **Generate candidates.** Use the three questions and the 15 model types to produce several candidate models, mixing revenue models within each (→ [creative-ideation](../creative-ideation/SKILL.md)). For an existing business, the current model is one candidate.
4. **Fill the canvas** in order 1–9 for the leading candidate(s). Tag every entry [A], [E], or [T].
5. **Check the system.** Run the Spirit Airlines consistency checks: everything points at the VP, every cost traces backstage, every segment has a channel, relationship, and revenue stream.
6. **Assess.** Score the seven factors with evidence; compare candidates; name the weakest factor and a redesign.
7. **Ripple-test** each proposed change: which other blocks must move?
8. **Risk check.** Set affordable loss; check commitments before revenue and term matching; classify the plan as smart or reckless.
9. **Plan the tests.** Pick the [T] entries that could sink the business and design the cheapest, most reversible test for each (→ [customer-validation](../customer-validation/SKILL.md)).
10. **Read results with a growth mindset and decide.** Ask "What did we learn? What will we try next?", locate the block that is actually failing, and apply the pre-written adapt / persist / stop criteria (→ [problem-statement](../problem-statement/SKILL.md) for the Proceed / Pivot / Punt decision). Ripple-test any pivot.
11. **Version it.** Increment the canvas version after each round of evidence and re-score.

### Deliverable

Unless the user asks for something narrower, deliver:
1. The one-paragraph text model with the Big Question answered.
2. The full canvas (Markdown template format), every entry status-tagged.
3. The assessment table with scores, evidence, total, and weakest factor.
4. The candidate comparison, if more than one model was considered.
5. The risk check and the top assumptions to test, each with a test and a decision rule.
6. A pointer to [assets/business-model-canvas.html](assets/business-model-canvas.html) if the user wants to work the canvas visually.

## Common Mistakes

- **Product without a business model.** Building an innovative product and assuming the business follows. A value proposition is necessary, not sufficient.
- **Treating a filled-in box as a fact.** Filling in a box does not prove it is true; untagged canvases hide which entries are guesses.
- **One revenue model by default.** Most strong businesses mix models; failing to ask "what else could earn here?" leaves recurring revenue and earlier cash on the table.
- **"Everyone" as the customer segment.** Segments that need different channels, relationships, or prices must be separated (→ [customer-segmentation](../customer-segmentation/SKILL.md)).
- **Blurring resources and activities.** Resources are what we need; activities are what we must do.
- **Backstage that doesn't serve the VP.** Activities, resources, or partners that do not trace to the value proposition are cost without purpose.
- **Ignoring channel economics.** On a $100 Nike shoe the retailer margin is $50 and Nike's profit is $5; channel choice can matter more than production cost.
- **Missing channel phases.** Planning awareness and purchase but not delivery or after-sales support.
- **Cost structure that contradicts the VP.** A "cheap, no frills" promise with personal-assistance relationships and value-driven costs.
- **Asset vs. liability mismatch.** Long fixed commitments funded by short, cancellable revenue (the WeWork pattern).
- **"Scale fast, profitability will follow."** Growth multiplies a broken unit economics; it does not fix it.
- **Overestimated markets.** A huge market number is not evidence of demand (→ [market-research](../market-research/SKILL.md)).
- **Betting more than the affordable loss.** Any plan whose downside is "lose everything" is reckless regardless of the upside.
- **Changing one block in isolation.** Every pivot or optimization ripples through other blocks; run the ripple test.
- **Assessment scores without evidence.** A 9 on "Protection from Competition" with no competitive analysis is an assumption, not a score.
- **A static canvas.** No version number, no re-scoring after new evidence.
- **Fixed-mindset reading of a bad result.** "The market doesn't get it" or "we're not cut out for this" instead of "what did we learn, and what will we try next?"
- **Pivoting the whole model when one block failed.** Airbnb's problem was execution (listing photos), not the idea; find the failing block first.
- **No persist / pivot / stop criteria before testing.** Without pre-written criteria, sunk cost and ego make the call instead of evidence.

## Worked Examples

### Positive Example: Water-Efficiency Retrofits for Older Apartments

**Situation.** A founder with a plumbing-trade background sees many 1970s–80s apartment complexes in a mid-size city. These buildings are very inefficient with water, and owners of complexes billed on a single master water meter pay that bill themselves. All figures below are the founder's planning assumptions, tagged as such.

**Step 1: Compare candidates** (the four models from the slides):

| Candidate | How it makes money | Assessment total | Weakest factor | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Matchmaker website | Referral/listing fees | 27 / 70 | Protection from competition (2): easy to copy, unclear who pays | Reject |
| Consultant | Hourly/project fee | 24 / 70 | Scalability (2): founder's hours | Reject |
| Plumbing company | Per-job service sales | 21 / 70 | Recurring revenues (1), protection (2): crowded trade | Reject |
| **Shared savings + mix** | Share of measured savings, plus options below | **37 / 70** | Earning vs. spending (2) | **Choose, and design around the weak factor** |

**Step 2: Text model.**

```text
[Water-fixture retrofit with measured savings] at center
  - targeting [owners/managers of 100–300-unit complexes built before 1990 who pay one master water bill]
  - delivering [a lower water bill with no upfront capital: we are paid only out of verified savings]
  - through [direct founder-led sales at apartment-association events and referrals]
  - communicated via [monthly savings reports that show the owner's bill before and after]
  - generating [50% of measured savings for 36 months; outright purchase option; post-contract monitoring subscription]

Big Question: yes only if measured savings exceed fixture + install + measurement cost within the contract term. [T]
```

**Step 3: Canvas** (abridged to key entries):

| Block | Entries |
|---|---|
| 1. Customer Segments | [E] Owners of pre-1990 complexes on a master meter (confirmed in 14 owner interviews) · [A] Property-management firms running several such complexes |
| 2. Value Propositions | [A] Lower water bill with zero upfront capital · [A] Payment only from verified savings, so the owner carries no performance risk |
| 3. Channels | Awareness: [E] apartment-association meetings · Evaluation: [A] free 12-month bill review with savings estimate · Purchase: shared-savings contract · Delivery: installs by partner plumber · After sales: [A] monthly savings report |
| 4. Customer Relationships | Acquire: personal assistance (founder-led sale) · Retain: [A] automated monthly report, long-term engagement over 36 months |
| 5. Revenue Streams | [T] 50% of measured savings vs. a 12-month baseline, 36 months (profit sharing, recurring) · [A] Outright purchase at a fixed price for owners who prefer to pay upfront (asset sale) · [A] Monitoring subscription after the contract ends |
| 6. Key Resources | [A] Baseline-and-measurement method plus bill data (intellectual) · [A] Credit line to fund fixtures (financial) · Founder's sales skill (human) |
| 7. Key Activities | Bill auditing and baseline setting (problem solving) · Install project management · Monthly measurement and reporting |
| 8. Key Partners | [A] Licensed plumbing contractor (performs the install, a critical activity) · [A] Fixture supplier with volume pricing (supplies a critical resource) · [A] Apartment association (strategic alliance for awareness) |
| 9. Cost Structure | Variable: fixtures, partner install labor per unit · Fixed: founder salary, measurement software · Financing cost on the credit line · Cost-driven |

**Step 4: Assessment.**

| Factor | Score | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Switching Costs | 7 | 36-month contract [A] |
| Recurring Revenues | 8 | Savings share is monthly; outright purchases are the only transactional stream |
| Earning vs. Spending | 2 | Fixtures and labor are paid before any savings arrive |
| Game-Changing Cost Structure | 5 | Unknown vs. competitors until a competitive analysis is done [T] |
| Others Who Do the Work | 4 | Partner plumber does the install, but is paid |
| Scalability | 6 | Partner crews scale; sales are founder-limited |
| Protection from Competition | 5 | Measurement data and owner relationships, not yet proven |
| **Total** | **37 / 70** | Weakest: Earning vs. Spending |

**Step 5: Redesign around the weak factor and run the risk check.**
- Affordable loss: the founder caps the first test at the fixture and labor cost of **one 40-unit pilot building** and four months of time.
- The outright-purchase option and a modest signing deposit pull some cash forward (Earning vs. Spending lever).
- Term match: the credit line is drawn per building, only after a contract is signed, so no fixed commitment outlasts the revenue that pays for it.
- Riskiest assumption [T]: measured savings are large enough that a 50% share repays fixtures and install inside the contract. Test: the pilot building, metered for three months before and after. Decision rule written in advance: persist if payback is under 18 months, change the split or the fixture package if 18–30 months, stop if over 30.

**Why this is a good example:**
- It compares four candidate models with the same assessment instead of picking by instinct.
- It **mixes** three revenue models inside one business.
- Every entry carries a status tag, and the one that could sink the business is marked [T] and has a test with a pre-written decision rule.
- Backstage traces to the VP: "paid from verified savings" requires the measurement activity and the measurement resource, which both appear.
- It scores itself honestly (a 2 is a 2) and then redesigns around the weakest factor.
- The risk is calculated: affordable loss is set before acting, the pilot is reversible, and there is no long commitment ahead of revenue.

### Negative Example: E-Bike Subscription in a College Town

**Situation.** A founder plans month-to-month e-bike subscriptions for a college town. The founder's canvas, as written:

| Block | Founder's entry |
|---|---|
| 1. Customer Segments | Everyone in town who needs to get around |
| 2. Value Propositions | The best e-bikes at the cheapest price with an amazing app |
| 3. Channels | Instagram |
| 4. Customer Relationships | Great customer service |
| 5. Revenue Streams | $79/month, cancel anytime |
| 6. Key Resources | 300 e-bikes, bought upfront with a 5-year loan the founder personally guarantees, plus the founder's savings |
| 7. Key Activities | Marketing |
| 8. Key Partners | None |
| 9. Cost Structure | Bikes, app, marketing |

The founder's plan: "Students will love it. Move fast, grab the whole town before anyone copies us, profitability will follow."

**What is wrong, block by block:**
- **No status tags.** Every entry is written as fact; nothing is marked Assumed or Needs testing, so nothing will be tested.
- **Customer Segments: "everyone."** Students, commuters, and tourists need different channels, relationships, and prices; they are separate segments (→ [customer-segmentation](../customer-segmentation/SKILL.md)).
- **Value Proposition: superlatives, no "why us."** "Best and cheapest" is not a reason a specific customer chooses this over a used bike or the bus (→ [value-proposition](../value-proposition/SKILL.md)).
- **Channels cover one phase.** Instagram handles awareness. Nothing handles evaluation, delivery, or after sales: who fixes a flat at 8 a.m.?
- **Key Activities miss the real work.** Maintenance, charging, fleet logistics, and theft recovery are the activities that make the model work; "marketing" is not the hard part.
- **Key Partners: none.** No bike shop for repairs, no insurer, even though both supply critical activities and resources.
- **Cost Structure is incomplete.** Maintenance, theft, insurance, and loan interest are missing, so the Big Question (is the value worth the price?) cannot be answered.
- **Asset vs. liability mismatch.** A 5-year fixed loan funds revenue that can be cancelled monthly, and students leave every summer. This is the WeWork pattern at small scale.

**Assessment, honestly scored:** Switching Costs 1 (cancel anytime) · Recurring Revenues 6 (monthly, but seasonal churn) · Earning vs. Spending 0 (all 300 bikes bought before the first rider) · Cost Structure unknown, so unscored [T] · Others Who Do the Work 0 · Scalability 2 (every new rider needs a new bike bought upfront) · Protection from Competition 1. **Total 10 / 60 scored.**

**Risk verdict: reckless.** Ego-driven ("grab the whole town"), overconfident (no evidence behind any entry), gambling mentality, and a catastrophic downside: the personal guarantee means failure costs the founder everything.

**How to fix it:**
- Set affordable loss first, then start with a 25-bike pilot leased or financed per semester, not 300 bikes on a 5-year loan.
- Pick one segment with a real problem, for example students living beyond walking distance of campus without a car, and validate it (→ [customer-validation](../customer-validation/SKILL.md)).
- Sell prepaid semester plans with a deposit (Earning vs. Spending and Switching Costs levers) and pause pricing for summer.
- Partner with a local bike shop for maintenance (a key partner performing a critical activity) and add insurance to the cost structure.
- Tag every entry, mark the fatal ones [T], and test them before scaling.

**Why this is a bad example:** it treats a filled canvas as proof, picks one revenue model by default, leaves backstage blocks empty or generic, commits fixed long-term costs ahead of cancellable revenue, never asks the Big Question, and bets more than the founder can afford to lose.
