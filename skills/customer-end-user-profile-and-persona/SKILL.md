# Customer End User Profile & Persona

You are an expert in defining end users for new and existing ventures. Your goal is to help the user describe, in research-based detail, the specific person who will **use** their product (the **End User Profile**), and then find one real person who embodies that profile and can be interviewed on an ongoing basis (the **Persona**).

## Before You Start

Gather or confirm these before drafting anything. If something is missing, ask; if the user wants to move fast, draft with explicit assumptions and flag every one as a hypothesis.

1. **The product or idea** — what it is and what it does, in one or two sentences.
2. **The segment** — which defined segment (ideally the beachhead) this end user lives in (→ [customer-segmentation](../customer-segmentation/SKILL.md)). An end user profile without a segment has no boundary.
3. **The problem** — what problem or frustration the product addresses (→ [problem-statement](../problem-statement/SKILL.md)).
4. **What research exists** — interviews done, observations, data, existing customers. This decides whether the profile is still a hypothesis or already research-backed.
5. **Who uses vs. who pays** — is the end user also the buyer? If not, both need to be identified (→ [customer-ecosystem](../customer-ecosystem/SKILL.md)).
6. **The task mode** — starting fresh, auditing an existing profile/persona, revising after new interviews, or rebuilding after a pivot. Each needs the same structure; only the starting point differs.

## End User Profile

### Definition

An **End User Profile** is:

- A **research-based description of a specific user within a defined segment**.
- **Fictional** — a composite, research-based fictional customer, not a real individual.
- **The person who will *use* your product.** Not necessarily the person who pays for it.
- Something that **begins as a hypothesis and improves with interviews** (→ [customer-interview](../customer-interview/SKILL.md)). Version one is your best guess; every round of interviews should sharpen it.

Think of it as bringing the segment into focus. Before the profile exists, you can "generally see them and describe them" (the people standing on your beachhead) "but they are not in completely clear focus yet." The profile is the act of focusing.

### Why Identify the End User

End users have different:

- **Ages**
- **Goals**
- **Needs**
- **Aspirations**
- **Behaviors**
- **Motivations**
- **Interests**
- **Fears**, etc.

**You must identify these for *your* end user.** The logic chain:

1. **Different users = different needs.** A product shaped for one user's needs misfires for another's.
2. **Build for them, not for you.** Your own preferences are not evidence about the user.
3. **If you do not, you cannot sell.** A product not built around a specific user's needs has no one it clearly fits.
4. **If you can't sell…** the venture fails. The slides close the point with: *"The greatest teacher, failure is."* Better to learn it from the profile than from the market.

### The Profile Questions (Characteristics)

Answer every question for your end user. These are the core of the profile.

| # | Characteristic | What you're really pinning down |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Gender?** | Whether the user skews to one gender or spans both |
| 2 | **Age range?** | Life stage, which drives budget, habits, and priorities |
| 3 | **Income range?** | Ability to pay, and how much is *disposable* |
| 4 | **Geographic location?** | Where they are — regions, or proximity/routes for local businesses |
| 5 | **What motivates them?** | The underlying drive (prestige, savings, experiences, learning, family) |
| 6 | **What do they read? Websites?** | Where their attention goes — and therefore where you can reach them |
| 7 | **What job are they trying to get done?** | The task the product is hired for (→ [value-proposition](../value-proposition/SKILL.md)) |
| 8 | **What problem or frustration do they experience?** | The pain your product relieves (→ [problem-statement](../problem-statement/SKILL.md)) |
| 9 | **What is the main/general reason they are buying this product? Savings? Image? Peer pressure?** | The actual purchase driver, which is often not the functional one |
| 10 | **What makes them special and easily identifiable?** | The trait that lets you *find* them — where they gather, what marks them out |
| 11 | **Tell their story!!** | A short narrative tying all answers into one believable person |

**Add questions the product calls for.** The slide case studies extend the list when it matters: a luxury product adds **Affiliations** (e.g., country clubs); a food concept adds **Interests** (e.g., foodie culture, social media) and **Where do they eat?** (e.g., trendy, lively, "Instagrammable" restaurants and pop-ups). Keep the core 11 and add, never subtract.

### How to Answer Well

- **Be specific enough to act on.** "Male; semi-retired/retirees over 52; $500K+ income with large disposable" is a profile. "Adults who like boats" is not.
- **Answer the reason-to-buy honestly.** In the slides' luxury boat case the reason is **Image**, and the motivation is **Prestige**. In the doughnut case the reason is **"the experience beyond the food itself."** Neither is the obvious functional answer — that is the point of the question.
- **Make geography behavioral when it fits.** For a local shop, location can be "within 15 minutes of the store, or on route to work/activity" rather than a city name.
- **Make "special and easily identifiable" findable.** Good answers say where to locate them: "run in the same circles; own multiple homes" or "find them at pop-ups and food events."
- **Income should note disposable income** where it matters: "$50K+ (needs some disposable)."
- **Mark each answer's source** — research/interview vs. assumption — so you know what still needs testing.

### One Profile per Distinct User Group

Different product lines or segments can need separate profiles. In the slides' StanCraft case (a builder of wooden boats), a consultant was hired to identify **two** profiles: the **custom boat customer profile** and the **semi-custom boat customer profile**. If two groups would answer the profile questions differently, they get separate profiles.

## Persona

### Fictional to Real

The two tools are a pair, and the difference is the whole point:

| | End User Profile | Persona |
|---|---|---|
| **What it is** | Research-based **fictional** customer | A **real person** who represents the end user profile |
| **Can you talk to it?** | No | **Yes — someone you can interview** |
| **Job** | Describe who you're building for | Test that description against reality, continuously |

**Why a persona is needed:**

- **Validates assumptions** — every hypothesis in the profile gets checked against a real person (→ [customer-validation](../customer-validation/SKILL.md)).
- **Provides ongoing feedback** — not a one-time interview but a standing relationship as the product evolves.
- **Answers what customers' needs and wants are** — from the source, not from your guesses.

### How to Select a Persona

Three criteria, all required:

1. **Choose someone who fits the end user profile.** They should answer the profile questions the way the profile says.
2. **Must be accessible for ongoing conversations.** One interview is not enough; you need repeated access.
3. **Should be an enthusiastic/willing participant.** A reluctant persona gives thin, polite answers.

The selection test — the persona **answers the question:**

> **"If I only had one end user to represent my end user profile, WHO would it be?"**

**Note: if the end user is not the buyer, identify the buyer separately.** The persona represents the *user*; the person who pays may have different criteria and needs their own treatment (→ [customer-ecosystem](../customer-ecosystem/SKILL.md)).

### Build: The Persona Fact Sheet

Build the fact sheet by **interviewing the person** (→ [customer-interview](../customer-interview/SKILL.md)). It records:

| Field | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| **Name** | The real person's name |
| **Relevant context** | Age, occupation, family, where they live, background — whatever bears on the problem |
| **Goal and pain** | What they're trying to achieve and what hurts along the way (→ [value-proposition](../value-proposition/SKILL.md)) |
| **Current alternative** | What they use or do today instead of your product |
| **Decision/purchase criteria** | What they weigh when choosing — their purchase priorities |
| **Ecosystem roles** | The groups, places, and people around them, and the roles they play (→ [customer-ecosystem](../customer-ecosystem/SKILL.md)) |
| **Direct quotes** | Their own words, verbatim |
| **Open questions to test** | What you still don't know or haven't confirmed |
| **Details that affect the problem, adoption, purchase, or access** | Document anything that changes whether they have the problem, would adopt, would buy, or can be reached |

### What a Strong Persona Looks Like (Slide Case)

The slides' persona for Dividends360 (a dividend-portfolio tool positioned as "More Income, Less Effort") is a real person, **Scott Wagoner**, presented in three layers:

1. **Identity card + overview:** Name, Age (64), Occupation (CPA / trust administration and management / retired), Married, Lives in (SC; previously Fort Wayne, IN for 40+ years), and a paragraph overview: his professional background, his diverse portfolio with a focus on dividend-paying assets, that in retirement he actively seeks to optimize the portfolio and maximize passive income, that he reads Seeking Alpha and other investment sites, and that he pays for an investment-ideas subscription service (his current alternative).
2. **Personal:** Children, **Hobbies** (golf, boating, investing, pickleball, grandkids, giving), **Ecosystem** (golf club, sporting events, investor chat boards), **Motivations** (learning, providing for family, teaching others), **Income** ($200K+).
3. **Purchase priorities:** Simplicity and ease of use; efficient portfolio tracking; planning; idea generation and collaboration; comprehensive research; maximizing passive income.

Notice how the layers map to the fact sheet: context (layer 1–2), current alternative (the paid subscription), ecosystem (layer 2), and decision/purchase criteria (layer 3).

## Workflow

1. **Anchor to the segment.** Name the defined segment (or beachhead) this user belongs to (→ [customer-segmentation](../customer-segmentation/SKILL.md)). If there are several distinct user groups, plan one profile each.
2. **Draft the End User Profile as a hypothesis.** Answer all 11 profile questions plus any product-specific extras. Tag each answer as research or assumption.
3. **Tell their story.** Write the 4–6 sentence narrative. If you can't write it convincingly, the profile is still too vague.
4. **Check user vs. buyer.** If they differ, flag the buyer for separate identification (→ [customer-ecosystem](../customer-ecosystem/SKILL.md)).
5. **Interview to sharpen the profile.** Use interviews to replace assumptions with findings (→ [customer-interview](../customer-interview/SKILL.md)). Update the profile after each round.
6. **Select the Persona.** List real candidates who fit the profile. Keep only those who are accessible for ongoing conversation and enthusiastic/willing. Pick one with the test: "If I only had one end user to represent my profile, who would it be?"
7. **Build the Persona Fact Sheet** by interviewing that person. Capture direct quotes, current alternative, and decision/purchase criteria in their words.
8. **Reconcile.** Where the persona contradicts the profile, update the profile (it's research-based; the persona is research). Move resolved items out of "open questions."
9. **Keep the persona in the loop.** Return to them for ongoing feedback as the product, pricing, or positioning changes (→ [customer-validation](../customer-validation/SKILL.md)).

**Auditing an existing profile/persona:** run steps 2–9 as a checklist — any unanswered profile question, untagged assumption, persona who fails a selection criterion, or fact sheet missing quotes/current alternative/criteria is a gap to fix.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Profiling "everyone" | Different users = different needs; a profile that fits all fits none | Narrow to one user inside one defined segment |
| Building for yourself | Your taste isn't evidence; "build for them, not for you" | Source each answer from research or interviews |
| Treating the first draft as fact | The profile *begins* as a hypothesis | Tag assumptions; revise after every interview round |
| Skipping the reason-to-buy question or giving the functional answer only | Misses the real driver (image, prestige, experience, peer pressure, savings) | Ask directly why they buy, and probe beyond function |
| "Special and easily identifiable" left vague | You can't find or reach them | Name where they gather and what marks them out |
| Confusing profile and persona | Profile is fictional; persona is a real, interviewable person | Keep both, and link the persona to the profile it represents |
| Persona is a celebrity, an imagined person, or someone unreachable | Fails "accessible for ongoing conversations" | Pick someone you can actually talk to repeatedly |
| Persona is a reluctant favor (friend/relative who doesn't fit) | Fails "fits the profile" and "enthusiastic/willing" | Choose for fit and willingness, not convenience |
| Fact sheet without direct quotes or current alternative | No evidence and no sense of what you must beat | Record verbatim quotes and what they use today |
| Ignoring the buyer when the user doesn't pay | You build for the user but can't close the sale | Identify the buyer separately |
| One profile for two distinct groups | Blends different needs into a user who doesn't exist | Split, as with custom vs. semi-custom boat buyers |

## Worked Examples

### Positive Example — Mobile Dog-Grooming Scheduling App

**Context:** A founder is building a scheduling and route app for **owner-operator mobile dog groomers** (one person, one van). Segment: solo mobile groomers in fast-growing suburban metros. Six interviews completed.

```
END USER PROFILE — GroomRoute / Solo mobile groomers, suburban metros
Status: Partly validated (6 interviews)

Gender: Mostly female (5 of 6 interviewed)                     Source: interviews
Age range: 28-45                                                Source: interviews
Income range: $45K-$85K net; reinvests in van & equipment, little
  disposable — software spend must clearly pay back             Source: interviews
Geographic location: Suburban metros; work a 15-25 mile radius
  from home                                                     Source: interviews
What motivates them: Being their own boss; predictable income;
  getting home for family by evening                            Source: interviews
What they read / websites: Groomer Facebook groups, grooming
  trade forums, YouTube technique channels                      Source: interviews
Job to get done: Fill the week with booked appointments in a
  tight geographic loop                                         Source: interviews
Problem or frustration: Booking by text at night; no-shows;
  zig-zag routes burning fuel and time                          Source: interviews
Main reason they'd buy: Savings — time and fuel; fewer lost
  appointments                                                  Source: 4 of 6 interviews
Special & easily identifiable: Branded vans; very active in
  groomer Facebook groups; attend regional grooming shows       Source: interviews
Extras — tools today: paper calendar + texting + phone maps     Source: interviews

Their story: Dana left a salon three years ago to run her own van. She
grooms 6 dogs a day, books clients by text after her kids are asleep,
and loses about one appointment a week to no-shows. Her route is
whatever order clients replied in, so she crisscrosses town. She wants
a full, tight week and her evenings back.

End user = buyer? Yes (owner-operator pays).
Open assumptions: willingness to pay monthly vs. per booking; whether
clients will self-book online.
```

```
PERSONA FACT SHEET — represents: Solo mobile groomer profile
Selection check: Fits profile Y | Accessible Y (agreed to biweekly calls) | Enthusiastic Y
"If I only had one end user to represent my profile, it would be: Dana R."

Relevant context: 34, ex-salon groomer, 3 years running her own van,
  two kids, works a 20-mile radius
Goal: 30 booked grooms/week, home by 5:30 pm
Pain: Night-time text booking; ~1 no-show/week; inefficient routes
Current alternative: Paper calendar, texting, phone maps; tried a
  salon booking tool and quit (no route view)
Decision/purchase criteria (ranked): 1. Works one-handed on a phone
  2. Cuts no-shows (reminders, deposits) 3. Under ~$40/month
Ecosystem roles: Groomer Facebook group (peer advice — she copies what
  others use); regional grooming show (where she discovers tools);
  her clients (would need to accept online booking)
Direct quotes:
  - "I do my booking in bed at 10 pm. Every night."
  - "A no-show isn't just lost money, it's a hole in my route."
Open questions to test: Would clients pay a booking deposit?
Details affecting adoption/purchase: Won't adopt anything that needs
  a laptop; trusts peer recommendations over ads
Last conversation: [date]  Next check-in: [date + 2 weeks]
```

**Why this works:**

- **Every profile question is answered and sourced** — it's research-based, with assumptions isolated for testing.
- **Reason-to-buy is concrete** (savings of time and fuel, fewer lost appointments), and income notes the tight disposable budget, which directly shapes pricing.
- **"Special and easily identifiable" is findable** — branded vans, specific Facebook groups, grooming shows.
- **The story makes one believable person** out of the data.
- **User = buyer is stated explicitly**, so no separate buyer is missing.
- **The persona passes all three selection criteria** and is a real person with scheduled ongoing access.
- **The fact sheet has every field**: current alternative (and why a competitor failed), ranked criteria, ecosystem roles, verbatim quotes, open questions, and adoption details.

### Negative Example — Same Idea, Done Badly

```
END USER PROFILE — GroomRoute
Gender: Everyone
Age: 18-65
Income: Any
Location: USA
Motivation: Wants an easier life
Reads: The internet
Problem: Scheduling is hard
Why they buy: Because the app is great
Story: Pet groomers everywhere will love this because I would.

PERSONA: "Groomer Gary" — 40, loves dogs, wants more efficiency.
  (Imagined by the founder; no interview.)
Backup persona: founder's cousin (runs a hair salon, has never groomed a dog).
```

**Why this fails:**

- **No segment, no specificity** — "everyone, 18-65, any income, USA" ignores that different users have different needs; it can't guide building or selling.
- **Built for the founder, not the user** — "because I would" is the exact trap "build for them, not for you" warns against.
- **Reason-to-buy is circular** ("the app is great") — it never asks whether the driver is savings, image, peer pressure, or something else.
- **Nothing is identifiable or findable** — "reads the internet" gives no channel to reach anyone.
- **Not research-based and not tagged as hypothesis** — no interviews, no sources, no open questions.
- **"Groomer Gary" is not a persona** — a persona is a real person you can interview; Gary is just a second fictional profile.
- **The cousin fails selection** — doesn't fit the profile, and convenience is not a criterion.
- **No fact sheet** — no current alternative, decision/purchase criteria, ecosystem roles, or direct quotes.
- **Salon groomers vs. mobile groomers blended** — two user groups with different problems squeezed into one profile.
