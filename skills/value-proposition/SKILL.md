# Value Proposition

You are an expert in crafting and stress-testing value propositions for new and existing ventures. Your goal is to help the user answer the one question every buyer silently asks, **"Why choose you (us)?"**, with a specific, customer-centered, differentiated statement that sells an outcome, not a buzzword.

## Before You Start

A value proposition is the **starting point for a viable business**. Customers will not buy because you exist. They buy when they see a **better solution to a problem**, so you have to convince them.

Gather these inputs before drafting. If any is missing, ask for it or route the user to the owning skill:

| Input | Question to ask | If missing |
|---|---|---|
| **Target customer** | Who exactly is this for? | → [customer-segmentation](../customer-segmentation/SKILL.md) |
| **Problem / job** | What job are they trying to get done, and what goes wrong? | → [problem-statement](../problem-statement/SKILL.md) |
| **Current alternatives** | What do they use today instead (including doing nothing)? | → [market-research](../market-research/SKILL.md) |
| **Product / service** | What do you actually offer? | Ask the user |
| **Evidence** | What have real customers said or done? | → [customer-interview](../customer-interview/SKILL.md) |

Work with whatever stage the user is at: a blank page, an existing tagline to audit, a pitch to optimize, or a VP that needs revising after a pivot.

## What a Value Proposition Is

### The Definition

In simple terms: **a value proposition is a statement that explains what benefit you provide, for who, and how you do it uniquely well.**

Three load-bearing words:

- **Benefit**: the result the customer gets, not the thing you built.
- **For who**: a specific target customer, not "everyone."
- **Uniquely**: something they cannot get, or cannot get as well, elsewhere.

### The Formula

```
Target Customer + Problem/Job + Meaningful Benefit + Differentiator = Value Prop
```

| Component | What it answers | Weak version | Strong version |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Target Customer** | For whom? | "Businesses," "everyone" | A specific, reachable group (→ [customer-segmentation](../customer-segmentation/SKILL.md)) |
| **Problem / Job** | What are they trying to do, and what hurts? | Implied or missing | Named job + named pain (→ [problem-statement](../problem-statement/SKILL.md)) |
| **Meaningful Benefit** | What result do they get? | A feature list | A concrete outcome they care about (time, money, ease, feeling) |
| **Differentiator** | Why you and not the alternative? | "Innovative," "cheaper" | A specific advantage over what they use today |

### The Buyer's Three Questions

Before buying anything, people ask themselves:

1. **Why this product?**
2. **Why this company?**
3. **Why this price?** (How the number itself is set: → [pricing-models](../pricing-models/SKILL.md).)

A strong VP answers all three. If it only answers the first, the customer may still buy the category from someone else.

## Questions Your VP Must Answer

Use this list to draft or audit. Every answer should be specific enough that a stranger could repeat it.

1. **What problem do we solve for customers?**
2. **What makes our solution different?**
3. **How do we compare with alternatives?**
4. **Can they get this elsewhere (and why not)?**

The question underneath all four: **What makes us unique?**

```
VP QUESTION SHEET
Problem we solve:            ______________________________________
For (specific customer):     ______________________________________
What makes us different:     ______________________________________
Main alternative(s) today:   ______________________________________
How we compare:              ______________________________________
Why they can't get this elsewhere: ________________________________
=> What makes us unique (one line): _______________________________
```

## How People Make Buying Decisions

Buying decisions happen on two levels at once. A VP that speaks to only one leaves the sale on the table.

### Logical Level ("Rational Brain")

Logical aspects focus on **product features** and answer the question **"Does this 'work' for me?"**

- Product specifications
- Warranty
- Price
- Color
- Size

### Emotional Level ("Feeling Brain")

Emotional aspects are **less tangible**, but they move people:

- Feelings of success
- Relief
- Pride
- Joy
- Fear

### Logical vs. Emotional in Practice

Slide example, a person buying a pair of jeans:

| Logical | Emotional |
|---|---|
| Waist size | How they look |
| Inseam length | How they make you feel |
| Color | Confident... and other feelings |
| Style | |

**Logical matters, but emotional often WINs the argument.** The logical side gets you into the consideration set ("these fit"). The emotional side closes the decision ("I feel confident in these"). Build the VP to clear the logical bar, then win on the emotional one.

```
LOGICAL / EMOTIONAL MAP
Does it "work" for them? (specs, price, warranty, size, fit):
  - ______________________
  - ______________________
How do they want to FEEL after choosing you? (success, relief, pride, joy, less fear):
  - ______________________
  - ______________________
Which emotion is the strongest lever for THIS customer? ______________
```

For grounding emotional drivers in a real buyer rather than a guess, build an end user profile or persona (→ [customer-end-user-profile-and-persona](../customer-end-user-profile-and-persona/SKILL.md)).

## Customer Jobs, Pains, and Gains

How to create a VP: **think about customers**, not your product. Work through three layers in order.

### 1. Jobs

The **jobs they are doing with your product/service**. A job is the task, goal, or outcome the customer is trying to accomplish (get from A to B, get paid, feed the family, ship a package on time).

### 2. Problems (Pains) With Those Jobs

What makes the job painful today. The slides name three core pain types:

| Pain type | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Costly** | It takes too much money to get the job done |
| **Hard** | It takes too much effort, skill, or coordination |
| **Frustrating** | It is annoying, uncertain, slow, or full of friction |

The underlying problem and its validation belong to → [problem-statement](../problem-statement/SKILL.md); here you use those pains to shape the benefit.

### 3. Benefits (Gains) the Customer Receives

What gets better when they use you. The slides name three core gain types:

| Gain type | Mirrors which pain |
|---|---|
| **Saving $** | Costly |
| **Saving time** | Hard / frustrating (slow) |
| **Doing the task easier** | Hard / frustrating |

### Putting It Together

Slide example: **Uber solves finding a ride by providing reliable, convenient transportation with less uncertainty/friction.** Job = finding a ride. Pain = uncertainty and friction. Benefit = reliable, convenient transportation.

```
JOBS -> PAINS -> GAINS
Customer: __________________________

Job they are doing:        ______________________________________
Pain: costly?              ______________________________________
Pain: hard?                ______________________________________
Pain: frustrating?         ______________________________________
Gain: saves $?             ______________________________________
Gain: saves time?          ______________________________________
Gain: makes it easier?     ______________________________________

One-line synthesis:
[Company] solves [job] by providing [benefit] with less [pain].
```

## Types of Benefits to Focus On

Once you know the gains, sort them into three categories. Strong VPs usually lead with one and support it with the others.

| Functional Benefits | Economic Benefits | Emotional / Social Benefits |
|---|---|---|
| Faster | Increase revenue | Feeling of success |
| More efficient | Decrease cost | Safety |
| Safer | Less turnover | Connected |
| etc. | More retention | etc. |
| | etc. | |

- **Functional** benefits answer "Does it work better?" (maps to the logical level).
- **Economic** benefits answer "Does it make or save me money?" (especially strong for business buyers).
- **Emotional / social** benefits answer "How will I feel, and how will others see me?" (maps to the emotional level, which often wins).

```
BENEFIT SORT
Functional (faster / more efficient / safer / ...):   ______________
Economic (revenue up / cost down / turnover down / retention up / ...): ______
Emotional-social (success / safety / connected / ...): ______________
Lead benefit (the one in the headline): ______________
```

## Differentiation: Uniqueness vs. Price

Ask: **How do you want customers to feel about choosing you?** The answer usually comes from uniqueness, not from price.

### Uniqueness

- **What makes you different?**
- **What can they get only from you?**

### Price

- **Offer other benefits beyond price.**
- **Low price is not usually a great strategy.** A competitor can always cut deeper, and price alone gives the customer no reason to stay.

**Being cheaper does not equal being differentiated.**

When price is part of the story, the pricing model has to match the VP: value-based pricing captures differentiated value, prestige pricing makes the price itself part of the value proposition, and pricing a lot lower than rivals raises a quality question (→ [pricing-models](../pricing-models/SKILL.md)).

When price *is* part of the story, pair it with something else. Compare two slide examples:

- **Target**, "Expect More, Pay Less": the uniqueness is that **you don't have to compromise on price or quality; you can get both.** Price is paired with quality.
- **Walmart**, "Save Money. Live Better": the focus is the **money aspect of the transaction**, but it **also highlights the intrinsic benefits** (a better life). Price is paired with an emotional outcome.

To check your differentiator against what customers actually use today, run a competitive analysis (→ [market-research](../market-research/SKILL.md)). Always ask the slide question: **What alternative is this replacing or improving?**

```
DIFFERENTIATION CHECK
Main alternative the customer uses today: ____________________
What we do that it doesn't (or does worse): ____________________
What they can get ONLY from us: ____________________
If price is part of it, what is it paired with? ____________________
Would this still be a reason to choose us if a rival matched our price? Y / N
```

## Features vs. Benefits

| Term | Definition | Example (invoicing tool) |
|---|---|---|
| **Feature** | An attribute of the product/service | "Automated invoice templates" |
| **Benefit** | The actual result the product/service provides | "Invoices sent in 2 clicks instead of 20 minutes" |

Customers buy benefits. Features belong in the proof, not the headline. To convert one into the other, ask **"so what?"** of each feature until you reach a result the customer feels.

### Reference Examples From the Slides

| Company: tagline | Product | Target market | Primary benefit | What makes it unique |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Stripe**: Web and mobile payments, built for developers | Tools that help empower businesses accept online payments | Developers and business owners | Simple, streamlined payments | Emphasis on simplicity |
| **Lyft**: Rides in Minutes | On-demand car service | People who need to get from point A to point B with on-demand service | Immediate response | Focus on providing service without any delays |
| **Uber**: Get there: Your day belongs to you | On-demand car service | People who need to get from point A to point B with on-demand service | Eliminates the frustrations of travel | Focus on the needs of the customer by using the word "you" |
| **Target**: Expect More, Pay Less | Thousands of SKUs across many industries | Cost-conscious shoppers who expect quality | Quality products at prices lower than the competition | You don't have to compromise on price or quality; you can get both |
| **Walmart**: Save Money. Live Better | Virtually any product a customer wants | Lower and middle-class bargain shoppers | Get the things that will make your life better, without the heavy price tag | Focus on the money aspect, while also highlighting intrinsic benefits |
| **Spotify**: Music for Everyone | Online streaming music | Families and single people alike | Music on the go | Moms, dads, kids, singles... music to suit everyone's tastes, all in one app |

Two shorter slide examples, stated as benefit bundles:

- **Chipotle**: fresh ingredients, customization, transparent ingredient sourcing.
- **FedEx**: fast delivery, by a specific time, which makes life much easier for its customers.

**Lessons from the set:**

- **Same product, same target, different VP.** Lyft and Uber sell the same thing to the same people. Lyft wins on a functional benefit (immediate response); Uber wins on an emotional one (your day belongs to you, no travel frustration). The VP is where you choose your battle.
- **Price-led brands still pair price with something.** Target pairs it with quality, Walmart with a better life.
- **These are mature, mass-market brands.** Taglines like "Music for Everyone" work for an incumbent with scale. A new venture needs a far more specific target customer (→ [customer-segmentation](../customer-segmentation/SKILL.md)).

## Common Mistakes

The slides name four, plus one from the differentiation section:

1. **Vague statements.** Words like **unique, innovative, disruptive, technology advanced** say nothing a customer can verify. Replace each with the concrete result it supposedly produces.
2. **Describing the benefits for the wrong person (not the customer).** "Scalable platform," "high-margin model," and "our team's expertise" are benefits to you or your investors, not to the buyer.
3. **Describing benefits customers do not care about.** A real benefit that doesn't touch their job or pain is noise. Interviews tell you which ones matter (→ [customer-interview](../customer-interview/SKILL.md)).
4. **Listing features instead of benefits.** Features are attributes; benefits are results. Lead with the result.
5. **Treating cheaper as differentiated.** Being cheaper does not equal being differentiated; low price is not usually a great strategy.

### Slide Rewrite: Buzzwords to Outcome

- **Before:** "We're an innovative, disruptive technology platform for invoicing in small business."
- **After:** "For small businesses, we automate invoicing in 2 clicks instead of 20 minutes; saving you time and money!"

What changed: the buzzwords (innovative, disruptive, technology platform) were replaced by a measurable outcome (2 clicks vs. 20 minutes) and the benefits customers care about (time and money), addressed to "you."

**Caveat:** "For small businesses" is *not* a specific customer or customer segment. This rewrite only illustrates being more specific and **selling the OUTCOME instead of spamming buzzwords**. A finished VP still needs a precise target customer (→ [customer-segmentation](../customer-segmentation/SKILL.md)).

## VP Quality Scorecard

Run every draft through this before calling it done.

| # | Check | Pass? |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Names a **specific** target customer (not "everyone," not "small businesses") | |
| 2 | Names the **job** and the **pain** (costly, hard, or frustrating) | |
| 3 | States a **benefit** (result), not a feature | |
| 4 | Benefit is one **this customer** cares about, not one for you or investors | |
| 5 | Has a **differentiator** tied to a named alternative | |
| 6 | Differentiator is **not price alone** | |
| 7 | Contains **zero** vague buzzwords (unique, innovative, disruptive, technology advanced) | |
| 8 | Clears the **logical** bar ("does it work for me?") | |
| 9 | Lands an **emotional** benefit (success, relief, pride, joy, safety, less fear) | |
| 10 | Answers **why this product, why this company, why this price** | |
| 11 | Concrete and measurable where possible (numbers, time, money) | |
| 12 | Customer-centered language ("you"), as in Uber's VP | |

## Workflow

1. **Collect inputs.** Target customer, problem/job, current alternatives, product, and any customer evidence (see Before You Start). Route gaps to the owning skill.
2. **Map jobs, pains, and gains.** Fill the Jobs → Pains → Gains sheet. Classify each pain as costly, hard, or frustrating.
3. **Sort benefits.** Place each gain in functional, economic, or emotional/social. Pick one **lead benefit**.
4. **Map logical vs. emotional.** Define what proves it "works" and what the customer should feel. Choose the strongest emotional lever.
5. **Find the differentiator.** Name the main alternative, what you do that it doesn't, and what they can get only from you. Reject price-only answers.
6. **Draft with the formula.** Target Customer + Problem/Job + Meaningful Benefit + Differentiator. Write two or three variants.
7. **Answer the four questions.** Problem solved, what's different, comparison to alternatives, why they can't get it elsewhere.
8. **Score and cut.** Run the VP Quality Scorecard. Strip buzzwords, convert features to benefits, remove benefits aimed at the wrong person.
9. **Deconstruct the result.** Fill the VP Deconstruction Card for your own VP and for one or two competitors, side by side.
10. **Test with real customers.** Put the VP in front of the target customer and see if it convinces them (→ [customer-validation](../customer-validation/SKILL.md)). Revise whenever the customer, problem, or alternatives change.

## Worked Examples

### Positive Example (Strong)

**Context:** A mobile app for owner-operated residential HVAC companies.

> For owner-operated HVAC companies running 2 to 10 field techs, where the owner spends evenings turning paper job tickets into invoices, FieldClose lets the tech send a signed invoice from their phone before leaving the customer's driveway, so you get paid days sooner and get your evenings back. Unlike generic invoicing apps that still need someone at a desk to retype the ticket, FieldClose builds the invoice from the job itself.
>
> Tagline: **"Invoice from the driveway. Get your evenings back."**

**Why it works:**

| Element | How it shows up |
|---|---|
| **Specific target customer** | Owner-operated residential HVAC, 2 to 10 field techs: a reachable, recognizable group, unlike "small businesses" |
| **Job** | Getting paid for completed service calls |
| **Pains** | Costly (slow payment ties up cash), hard (retyping tickets), frustrating (evenings lost to paperwork) |
| **Benefit, not feature** | "Signed invoice before leaving the driveway," not "mobile invoicing module" |
| **Benefit mix** | Functional (faster), economic (paid sooner, better cash flow), emotional (relief: evenings back) |
| **Logical + emotional** | Logic: it works in the field. Emotion: relief and time with family, which often wins |
| **Differentiator vs. named alternative** | Generic invoicing apps that still need desk re-entry |
| **Not price-led** | No "cheapest" claim; the reason to choose survives a rival's price cut |
| **No buzzwords; "you" language** | Zero instances of innovative / disruptive / AI-powered |

Note: claims like "paid days sooner" must be validated with real customers before going public (→ [customer-validation](../customer-validation/SKILL.md)).

### Negative Example (Weak)

**Context:** A meal-planning app.

> MealMind is an innovative, AI-powered, disruptive meal-planning platform for everyone. With 10,000+ recipes, smart grocery lists, macro tracking, and a sleek interface, our scalable technology is positioned to capture a massive market, all at the lowest price on the market.

**Why it fails:**

| Mistake | Where it shows up |
|---|---|
| **Vague statements** | "Innovative," "AI-powered," "disruptive," "technology" say nothing verifiable |
| **No specific target customer** | "For everyone": no one feels spoken to |
| **No job or pain** | Never says what the customer is struggling with (no time to plan? hitting a fitness goal? feeding picky kids?) |
| **Features instead of benefits** | 10,000 recipes, grocery lists, macro tracking, sleek interface are all attributes, not results |
| **Benefits for the wrong person** | "Scalable" and "capture a massive market" are investor benefits, not customer benefits |
| **Price as the differentiator** | "Lowest price on the market": cheaper does not equal differentiated |
| **No alternative named** | Never says what it replaces or improves (other apps, a notebook, takeout) |
| **No emotional benefit** | No relief, pride, confidence, or success; nothing for the "feeling brain" |

**Fix direction:** pick one customer (for example, parents of young kids with weeknight dinner stress), name their job and pain, lead with the outcome they feel, and differentiate against the alternative they actually use today.
